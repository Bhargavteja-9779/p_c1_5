# Highlights {.unnumbered}
- Conformal RT windows keep 90 % only on average; a third of laboratories fall below
- Replaying a laboratory's calibrant protocol on public LC systems calibrates its window
- Guarantee: ≥90 % of correct structures retained in ≥90 % of laboratories
- Finite guaranteed windows from three calibrants and for non-random calibrant mixes
- In 37 NORMAN laboratories, current RT filters silently lose correct structures

# Abstract {.unnumbered}
**Background.** Retention-time (RT) filters discard candidate structures in LC–HRMS non-target screening (NTS), but their tolerance windows are heuristic. Even conformal windows, which guarantee a retention rate, do so only on average over laboratories: an individual laboratory cannot know whether its own filter keeps the correct structures. We aimed at RT windows with a guarantee for the laboratory itself, from a handful of calibrants.

**Results.** We introduce cross-system conformal calibration (XSC). The laboratory's calibrants place and scale the window, with the scale shrunk towards a meta-learned difficulty model, and the window width is calibrated by replaying the laboratory's calibrant protocol on hundreds of public chromatographic systems. On 228 reversed-phase systems in leave-source-out validation with 20 calibrants, jackknife+ windows met the average target but 35 % of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of correct structures (48 % with ±z·RMSE windows). With XSC, 8.0 % did (target ≤ 10 %), with windows 0.80 times as wide as equally guaranteed jackknife+ windows. The control held with three calibrants, for RT-spanning, common or polar calibrant mixes, and in 37 external NORMAN interlaboratory methods (9 % failing). In a real-world case study, the laboratories used their own calibration mixes to screen suspects against PubChemLite isomers. Current windows lost the correct structure in 35 %–59 % of laboratories. XSC without the meta-learned scale retained 97 % of correct structures (8 % of laboratories below 90 %).

**Significance.** XSC turns RT filtering into a procedure with a stated, laboratory-level error rate: at least 90 % of correct structures retained, with 90 % confidence, for the laboratory's actual calibrants. It needs only calibrant structures and RTs, routine metadata and public data, and it shows before measurement which calibrant designs are informative.

**Keywords:** non-target screening; retention-time prediction; conformal prediction; uncertainty quantification; compound identification; LC–HRMS



# Introduction

Non-target screening (NTS) by liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS) detects thousands of features per sample, but turning a feature into a structure remains the bottleneck of exposomics, environmental monitoring and metabolomics [@hupatz2025critical]. Accurate mass and MS/MS spectra typically leave several isomeric candidate structures, so orthogonal evidence is needed [@schymanski2014identifying]. Retention time (RT) is the most widely available such evidence: a candidate whose expected RT is incompatible with the observed RT of the feature can be discarded [@hupatz2025critical; @metz2025identification].

Using RT this way requires predicting it on the laboratory's own chromatographic system, for which training data are rarely available. Public RT knowledge is therefore transferred with a small set of calibrant compounds measured on that system, either by *projection* through a monotone relation fitted on shared compounds [@stanstrup2015predret; @abatepella2015retention] and calibrant-based retention indices [@aalizadeh2021development; @stoffel2022alkylpyridinium], or by structure-based *prediction* models trained on large public collections [@domingo2019smrt; @kretschmer2024report] and adapted to the target system [@bouwmeester2020generalized; @garcia2022probabilistic; @fadil2026fastret; @xiong2026unirt]. Because elution order is far better conserved between systems than absolute RT, retention-order models learned across many systems [@bach2018retention; @kretschmer2026order] followed by a calibrant mapping now transfer without target training data. Their accuracy, however, depends strongly on the similarity of source and target systems [@malm2025projection].

For candidate filtering, the point prediction is only half of the story: a *tolerance window* must be set around it, and here practice is heuristic — fixed windows (±0.1–0.5 min [@metz2025identification], ±0.40 min [@kretschmer2026order], ±3 min [@mceachran2018comparison]), fractions of the run time [@mceachran2018comparison], or multiples of the calibration error such as ±2 RMSE [@malm2025projection]. Projection-specific windows had to be relaxed by 50 % to suit all of seven laboratories [@boswell2015calculation], and where the fraction of correct structures surviving the filter was measured, losses were substantial: 21–41 % on four external methods [@domingo2019smrt], 6–20 % after calibration with 35 calibrants [@zhang2024generic]. The asymmetry of the two errors makes this important: a window that is too wide keeps some wrong candidates, but one that is too narrow silently removes the right answer, which no downstream score can recover [@metz2025identification].

Conformal prediction offers prediction intervals with a finite-sample coverage guarantee for any underlying model, assuming exchangeable data [@vovk2005algorithmic; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]; it has recently been used for spectroscopic calibration [@wiens2026calibrated; @bhargav2026group], MS/MS retrieval [@rakhshaninejad2026reliable] and collision-cross-section (CCS) candidate prioritisation in NTS [@ferrandoplo2026candidate]. For RT on a laboratory's own system, conformal windows can be built from the calibrants themselves, e.g. by jackknife+ [@barber2021jackknife]. Two properties of such windows, however, make their guarantee weaker in practice than it reads. First, it is *marginal*: it averages over all laboratories and over all calibrant sets a laboratory might have drawn. The retention achieved by one laboratory with its actual calibrants fluctuates around the nominal level, and a guarantee for the laboratory's own calibrant set (training-conditional or PAC validity) needs hundreds of calibration points when the calibration set is the only source of information [@vovk2013conditional; @bian2023training]. Second, conformal windows from $n$ calibrants are finite only if $n\ge 1/\alpha-1$, and they are valid only if the calibrants resemble the analytes, whereas laboratories use few calibrants, chosen by availability, frequency of use or a fixed standard mix [@aalizadeh2021development; @malm2024norman].

Both limitations stem from treating the laboratory's calibrants as the only calibration data. Public repositories, however, now contain RTs measured on hundreds of chromatographic systems [@kretschmer2024report]. On every such system the laboratory's calibrant protocol — how many calibrants, chosen by which rule — can be replayed, and the retention it would have achieved there can be measured on the system's remaining compounds. Treating *systems*, rather than compounds, as the exchangeable calibration units turns the question "which window multiplier gives this laboratory at least $1-\varepsilon$ retention with confidence $1-\delta$?" into an ordinary conformal quantile over hundreds of systems. Related constructions exist in machine learning — PAC prediction sets for meta-learning [@park2022pac], hierarchical conformal prediction [@dunn2023distribution; @lee2026hierarchical] and few-shot conformal prediction with auxiliary tasks [@fisch2021fewshot; @park2024fewshot] — but, to our knowledge, they have not been developed for chromatographic retention or used to give a laboratory a guarantee about its own RT filter.

Here we develop *cross-system conformal calibration* (XSC) of RT windows and evaluate it as an analytical tool. Specifically, we (i) show on 228 reversed-phase systems from RepoRT, in leave-source-out validation, that windows with a marginal guarantee — including conformal jackknife+ windows — leave about a third of laboratories below the intended retention with 20 calibrants; (ii) derive XSC windows whose multiplier is calibrated on public systems with the laboratory's own protocol replayed, which guarantee that a laboratory retains at least $1-\varepsilon$ of the correct structures with probability at least $1-\delta$, remain finite with as few as three calibrants, and stabilise the laboratory's error scale by shrinkage towards a meta-learned difficulty model; (iii) show that replaying the calibrant-selection rule keeps the guarantee when calibrants are not representative (RT-spanning, most common or most polar compounds); and (iv) quantify the analytical consequence on real candidate lists — all isomers of each analyte in PubChemLite for Exposomics [@schymanski2021pubchemlite] — and on 37 NTS methods of the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison [@malm2025projection], including their own calibration mix. A related method of ours for near-infrared calibration under deployment shift [@bhargav2026group] calibrates intervals on labelled samples from each new group; it does not address chromatographic transfer, laboratory-level guarantees or candidate filtering.


# Theory and calculation

## Problem formulation

Let $T$ denote the chromatographic system used in a non-target screening (NTS) study and $t(x)$ the retention time (RT) of compound $x$ on $T$. A laboratory measures a set of $n$ calibrants $\mathcal{D}_n=\{(x_i,t_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ on $T$ and has access to public RT data acquired on other systems. A candidate structure $x$ proposed for an LC-HRMS feature with observed retention time $t_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is retained if $t_{\mathrm{obs}}\in C(x)$, where $C(x)$ is an RT window. For annotation, what matters is the fraction of *correct* structures that survive the filter. We call it the *retention* of the laboratory,
$$\mathrm{cov}_T(C)=\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\mathcal{A}_T}\{t(x)\in C(x)\mid\mathcal{D}_n\},$$
where $\mathcal{A}_T$ is the population of analytes on $T$. Its complement is the false-rejection rate of the filter. The retention is a random quantity: it depends on the system and on the calibrants the laboratory happened to measure. A *marginal* (average) guarantee controls its expectation, $\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{cov}_T(C)\ge 1-\alpha$. A *laboratory-level* guarantee controls the probability that the laboratory's own retention is adequate,
$$\mathbb{P}\{\mathrm{cov}_T(C)\ge 1-\varepsilon\}\ \ge\ 1-\delta ,$$
so that at most a fraction $\delta$ of laboratories (or of calibrant sets) fall below $1-\varepsilon$. The second goal is to eliminate as many incorrect candidates as possible, which requires windows that are as narrow as the guarantee permits.

## Base retention-order score

Absolute RTs change strongly between systems, whereas elution order is far better conserved [@kretschmer2026order]. We therefore learn, as in earlier multi-system retention-order models [@bach2018retention; @kretschmer2026order], a system-independent score $s(x)$ whose ordering agrees with the elution order observed within each training system; the base model is not a contribution of this work, and any model producing a score could be used. Training data are all RP systems from sources other than that of $T$ (plus a 15,000-compound subsample of the METLIN SMRT system [@domingo2019smrt]). A gradient-boosted ranking model (XGBoost, pairwise objective with query groups = chromatographic systems [@chen2016xgboost]) is fitted on 217 RDKit two-dimensional descriptors and a 1024-bit Morgan count fingerprint (radius 2) [@rdkit; @rogers2010extended]. Because only the order of $s$ is used, no per-system normalisation of RTs is required.

## Monotone calibrant mapping

On the target system the score is mapped to time by a monotone function $g$ fitted on the calibrants: isotonic regression of $t_i$ on $s_i$, followed by piecewise-cubic Hermite (PCHIP) interpolation through the isotonic fit [@fritsch1980monotone], and linear extrapolation beyond the calibrant score range with the Theil–Sen slope of the calibrants. Monotonicity encodes the assumption that the base score preserves elution order on $T$; deviations from this assumption (selectivity changes) appear as residuals and are absorbed by the window. Leave-one-out (LOO) maps $g_{-i}$ are obtained by refitting without calibrant $i$.

## Conformal windows from the calibrants alone

*Jackknife+.* With LOO residuals $R_i=|t_i-g_{-i}(s_i)|$, define for a compound $x$ the $n$ values $v^{\mathrm{hi}}_i(x)=g_{-i}(s(x))+R_i$ and $v^{\mathrm{lo}}_i(x)=g_{-i}(s(x))-R_i$. The jackknife+ window [@barber2021jackknife] is
$$C_\alpha(x)=\left[\,v^{\mathrm{lo}}_{(k_{\mathrm{l}})}(x),\ v^{\mathrm{hi}}_{(k_{\mathrm{u}})}(x)\,\right],$$
where $v_{(k)}$ denotes the $k$-th smallest of the $n$ values, $k_{\mathrm{u}}$ is $(1-\alpha)(n+1)$ rounded up and $k_{\mathrm{l}}$ is $\alpha(n+1)$ rounded down. If calibrants and the compound of interest are exchangeable draws from the compounds analysed on $T$, then $P\{t(x)\in C_\alpha(x)\}\ge 1-2\alpha$ for any mapping algorithm that treats the calibrants symmetrically, as the monotone map does, and coverage is close to $1-\alpha$ when the fitted map is stable [@barber2021jackknife]. As for conformal prediction in general [@angelopoulos2023gentle; @bhargav2026group], the window is finite only if $k_{\mathrm{u}}\le n$, i.e.
$$n\ \ge\ \frac{1}{\alpha}-1,$$
so that at least 9 calibrants are needed for $\alpha=0.10$, 19 for $\alpha=0.05$ and 4 for $\alpha=0.20$.

*Normalised jackknife+ with a meta-learned difficulty model.* RT errors after transfer are heteroscedastic: they depend on the compound (e.g. ionisable groups whose charge state differs between mobile phases, compounds dissimilar to the training data [@matyushin2024uncertainty]) and on where the compound falls relative to the calibrants (interpolation versus extrapolation [@malm2025projection]). Normalising the nonconformity score by an estimate of the local difficulty is a standard way to obtain adaptive conformal intervals [@papadopoulos2011regression; @lei2018distribution]. Let $\sigma(x;\mathcal{D})>0$ be a difficulty (scale) function. With $\sigma_{-i}$ denoting the difficulty function computed with calibrant $i$ left out of $\mathcal{D}_n$ and normalised scores $R_i=|t_i-g_{-i}(s_i)|/\sigma_{-i}(x_i)$, the window is defined as above with
$$v^{\mathrm{hi}}_i(x)=g_{-i}(s(x))+\sigma_{-i}(x)R_i,\qquad v^{\mathrm{lo}}_i(x)=g_{-i}(s(x))-\sigma_{-i}(x)R_i.$$
Because $\sigma_{-i}$ is computed from the leave-one-out calibrant set by the same symmetric procedure that produces $g_{-i}$, the argument of Barber et al. [@barber2021jackknife, Thm. 1] applies verbatim to the scaled score, and the $1-2\alpha$ guarantee is retained (proof in Supplementary Section S2). The function $\sigma$ itself is learned only from other chromatographic systems and is therefore fixed with respect to the target data. Learning the scale or nonconformity function on related tasks has precedents in few-shot and meta-learned conformal prediction [@fisch2021fewshot; @park2024fewshot]; here, the related tasks are other chromatographic systems, and the scale function is conditioned on the calibrant geometry and on routinely reported system metadata.

## Meta-learned difficulty model

The difficulty model is $\sigma(x;\mathcal{D})=\exp\{h(\phi(x,\mathcal{D},z_T))\}+10^{-3}$ min, where $h$ is a gradient-boosted regression tree ensemble (LightGBM [@ke2017lightgbm; @lightgbm]) and $\phi$ concatenates five feature groups: (i) *physicochemical* descriptors (logP, TPSA, molecular weight, H-bond donors/acceptors, rotatable bonds, fraction sp³, ring counts, molar refractivity, Labute ASA, element counts); (ii) *ionisation* features (SMARTS counts of carboxylic, sulfonic and phosphonic acids, phenols, primary/secondary/tertiary aliphatic amines, quaternary nitrogen, amidines/guanidines, pyridine- and imidazole-type nitrogens, formal charge); (iii) *novelty* features (maximum Tanimoto similarity of $x$ to the base-model training compounds and an exact-match flag); (iv) *calibrant-geometry* features computed from $\mathcal{D}$ (position of $s(x)$ relative to the calibrant score range, extrapolation distance, gap to the nearest calibrant score, calibrant RT span, number of calibrants); and (v) *system* descriptors $z_T$ from routinely reported metadata (mobile-phase pH class, organic modifier, USP column class, column length, particle size, temperature, flow rate, gradient duration, dead time). $h$ is trained to predict $\log(|r|+0.01\ \mathrm{min})$ for transfer residuals $r$ generated by *simulating the calibrant protocol* on training systems: for each training system, random calibrant sets of size 10, 20 and 40 are drawn (three draws each), the monotone map is fitted, and residuals of all remaining compounds are recorded. To make these residuals representative of transfer to an unseen system, base scores for each training source group are produced by a base model trained without that group (inner cross-fitting, Fig. 1).

## Cross-system conformal calibration (proposed)

Both windows above give only a marginal guarantee, and both need $n\ge 1/\alpha-1$ calibrants. XSC removes these limitations by using the calibrants only to *place* and *scale* the window and by calibrating its width on other chromatographic systems (Fig. 1).

*Window.* With the monotone map $g$ fitted on all $n$ calibrants, the leave-one-out residuals $r_i=t_i-g_{-i}(s_i)$ and the difficulty model $\sigma$ of Section 2.5, the laboratory's error scale is estimated with shrinkage towards the difficulty model,
$$\hat\tau^2=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{r_i/\sigma_{-i}(x_i)\}^2+k}{n+k},$$
i.e. $k$ pseudo-calibrants whose normalised residual equals the meta-learned prediction ($k=5$, fixed a priori; $k=0$ and $\sigma\equiv1$ are reported as variants). The window is
$$C_c(x)=\left[\,g(s(x))-c\,\hat\tau\,\sigma(x),\ g(s(x))+c\,\hat\tau\,\sigma(x)\,\right].$$
The shrinkage matters when $n$ is very small: with three calibrants, a scale estimated from three residuals alone varies by orders of magnitude between laboratories.

*Replaying the protocol.* For each of $S$ training systems $s$, the laboratory's calibrant protocol — the same $n$ and the same selection rule $\rho$ (random, RT-spanning, most frequently measured, a given standard mix, …) — is replayed $R$ times. Base scores and $\sigma$ come from models that did not see $s$ (cross-fitting by source group). For every replay the remaining compounds of $s$ give the pivot $u(x)=|t(x)-g(s(x))|/\{\hat\tau\sigma(x)\}$. From the pivots follow the retention $\mathrm{cov}_{s}(c)$ achieved on $s$ with multiplier $c$ and the smallest multiplier $V_{s}(\varepsilon)$ that would have given $s$ a retention of at least $1-\varepsilon$.

*Calibration.* For a marginal guarantee, $c$ is the smallest value for which the mean retention over training systems reaches $1-\alpha$. For a laboratory-level guarantee, $c$ is the $\lceil(1-\delta)(S+1)\rceil$-th smallest of $V_1(\varepsilon),\dots,V_S(\varepsilon)$. If the target laboratory is exchangeable with the training systems, the ranks of $V_T(\varepsilon),V_1(\varepsilon),\dots,V_S(\varepsilon)$ are uniform and therefore
$$\mathbb{P}\{\mathrm{cov}_T(C_c)\ge 1-\varepsilon\}\ \ge\ 1-\delta$$
(Supplementary Section S7). The argument is the split-conformal argument applied to laboratories instead of compounds [@park2022pac; @dunn2023distribution]. It requires no assumption on the error distribution or on the quality of the base model, and none on $n$: three calibrants give a finite window. With $R>1$ replays per system we use the draw-averaged empirical distribution function of $V$, which gives multipliers within about 10 % of the exact rule and practically the same failure rates (Table S28, Section 4.2). The guarantee concerns the laboratory's actual calibrant set, which a calibrant-only method cannot provide with a few calibrants [@vovk2013conditional; @bian2023training]. Its price is an exchangeability assumption one level up: the laboratory's system and analytes must resemble public systems and their measured compounds. We test this assumption by holding out entire source groups and on external interlaboratory data.

*Selection awareness.* When the calibrants are not a random sample of the analytes, replaying the selection rule calibrates the window for exactly that protocol. A rule is replayable if it can be applied to any public system — for example "the 20 compounds measured most often in public data", "the 20 most polar compounds" or "the compounds of a given mix that were measured on the system". Selection-aware calibration of a jackknife+-shaped window towards a marginal target (Supplementary Section S6) is a special case.

## Comparator windows

All comparators use the same base score, the same calibrants and (where applicable) the same monotone map: (B1) Gaussian window $g(s(x))\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{LOO}}$, representing the common practice of setting the tolerance from calibration residuals; (B2) penalised cubic B-spline (P-spline [@eilers1996flexible]) mapping with the classical prediction interval $\hat t\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-\mathrm{edf}}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+h(x)}$ and smoothing parameter chosen by generalised cross-validation, analogous to the projection intervals of PredRet [@stanstrup2015predret]; (B3) Gaussian-process projection (RBF + linear + white-noise kernel) with Gaussian predictive intervals, with hyper-parameters fitted by maximising the marginal likelihood of the target calibrants — unlike the GP projection of García et al. [@garcia2022probabilistic], whose prior is meta-learned on other chromatographic methods; (B4) split conformal prediction [@papadopoulos2002inductive; @lei2018distribution] with half of the calibrants used for fitting and half for calibration; (B5) unnormalised jackknife+; (B6) jackknife+ normalised by the spread of five bootstrap-replicate base models (a standard heuristic uncertainty signal [@matyushin2024uncertainty]), computed leave-one-out consistently; the nearest-neighbour-similarity term foreseen for B6 in the analysis plan was not implemented; and (B7, post hoc) a GP projection whose hyper-parameters are meta-learned on the training systems, our implementation of the meta-learned-prior idea of García et al. [@garcia2022probabilistic]: a constant + linear + RBF kernel on the calibrant-standardised score and RT, with the five hyper-parameters chosen per fold to maximise the predictive log-likelihood of held-out compounds after simulated calibrant draws on the training systems (inner cross-fitting as for the difficulty model) and then applied without any tuning to the target systems. To separate the effect of the window shape from that of the cross-system calibration, (B8) jackknife+ windows whose scores are multiplied by a laboratory-level XSC multiplier (window shape of B5, calibration of XSC) and (B9) XSC with $\sigma\equiv1$ and $k=0$ (a calibrated $\pm c\cdot\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{LOO}}$ rule) were also evaluated.

# Experimental

## Data

RT data for model development and the main evaluation were taken from RepoRT v1.0 [@kretschmer2024report; @report2025zenodo], a curated repository of small-molecule RTs with standardised structures and chromatographic metadata (CC BY-SA 4.0; archive SHA-256 4eeeb46a…6091); the external validation used the NORMAN interlaboratory data [@malm2025projection] (Section 4.10). The target systems stem from the BGC data set of Harrieder and Witting deposited in RepoRT, the studies of Folberth et al. [@folberth2020ms2], Souihi et al. [@souihi2022multiconditionrt] and Stoffel et al. [@stoffel2022alkylpyridinium], the PredRet collection [@stanstrup2015predret] and further sources (Table 1). Compounds were identified by the connectivity block of the standard InChIKey; stereoisomer entries within a system were averaged when their RTs differed by ≤0.2 min and removed otherwise. Mobile-phase pH class and organic modifier were derived from the reported additives and solvents. Target systems were all reversed-phase (RP) systems with at least 60 compounds after cleaning, excluding METLIN SMRT, which served only as an auxiliary training library: 228 systems with 65,590 retention times (median 130.5 compounds per system; Table 1, Fig. S1). HILIC systems with ≥60 compounds were analysed separately as an out-of-mechanism test (37 systems).

## Evaluation protocol and leakage control

Systems were partitioned by data source into five leave-source-out folds (BGC: 66 systems; Folberth: 63 systems; Other sources: 22 systems; PredRet: 41 systems; Souihi+Stoffel: 36 systems). For each fold, the base model, the bootstrap ensemble (B6) and the difficulty model were trained exclusively on systems from the other four folds (and SMRT; numbers of training systems per fold in Table S1), so that no retention time or chromatographic system of a held-out source group entered model development, as required for a realistic estimate of performance on new systems [@aca2023validation] (compounds, by contrast, are shared across sources; see below). The only retention data taken from a target system were those of its calibrants; the difficulty model additionally used the system's reported chromatographic metadata. Within each target system, calibrant sets of size $n\in\{10,15,20,30,40\}$ were drawn uniformly at random (20 draws per $n$, fixed seeds), and all remaining compounds of the system formed the test set; windows were computed for $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}$ (P-spline, GP and ensemble-normalised windows only for $n\in\{10,20,40\}$, as pre-specified, because of their computational cost). The primary setting ($n=20$, $\alpha=0.10$), the comparators, metrics and statistical tests were specified in an analysis plan committed to version control before the full run (code/EXPERIMENT_PLAN.md); a preceding smoke test with small models on two systems per fold revealed one implementation error in comparator B6, which was corrected before the full run (plan addendum A). The plan was not registered with an external registry; the only deviation from it is the omitted similarity term of B6 (Section 2.7). Because many compounds of a target system also occur in public data from other laboratories (73 % of test compounds at $n=20$ were present in the base model's training data), test compounds were additionally stratified into those seen and not seen by the base model, and by their maximum Tanimoto similarity to its training set.

Pre-specified robustness experiments used $n=20$ and 10 draws per system with (i) calibrants spread over the RT range (one random compound per RT quantile stratum), (ii) the 20 compounds of each system most frequently measured across RepoRT, (iii) Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 2 % of the RT span added to the calibrant RTs and (iv) one calibrant shifted by 10 % of the span. Generalisation was assessed with a second base model (a LightGBM regressor trained on SMRT only) and on HILIC systems (base and difficulty models trained on HILIC systems from other sources), and sensitivity to the difficulty-model hyper-parameters by varying the number of leaves (15 or 63, with 400 trees) or of trees (200 or 800, with 31 leaves), with 10 draws per system. Analyses added after the plan was written but before the corresponding results were computed are labelled post hoc: fixed-tolerance windows on the same calibrant draws, very small calibrant sets ($n=5$ and 8), the external validation on NORMAN data, the selection-aware calibration and the meta-learned GP comparator B7 (plan addenda B–E; B7 was evaluated on the identical calibrant draws, verified by recomputing the jackknife+ coverage of the first draw at $n=20$).

## Cross-system calibration, candidate lists and external laboratories

XSC (Section 2.6; plan addendum G, committed before the run) was evaluated in the same leave-source-out folds. For each fold, the laboratory's protocol was replayed on every training system with at least 40 compounds (191–241 systems per fold; 8 replays per system and protocol). The replays used base scores from a model trained without the fold and without the system's own source group, and a difficulty model trained without that source group, so that no system calibrated itself. Protocols were $n\in\{3,5,10,20,40\}$ calibrants chosen at random, spread over the RT range, as the compounds measured most often in public data (counted over public systems outside the laboratory's source group), or as the most polar compounds (lowest computed logP; a deliberately unrepresentative rule). The multipliers were then applied unchanged to the held-out target systems, with the calibrant draws of the main experiment (20 draws per system for random calibrants, 10 otherwise). The laboratory-level target was $\varepsilon=0.10$, $\delta=0.10$, fixed before the run.

*Real candidate lists.* For each test compound, the candidate list was every structure in PubChemLite for Exposomics (version 2026-09-25, 469,602 structures [@schymanski2021pubchemlite; @pubchemlite2026zenodo]) with the same molecular formula and a different InChIKey connectivity block, as an accurate-mass or formula query would return. Lists were capped at 200 randomly chosen isomers per formula (median list size 15.0). A candidate was eliminated when the observed RT of the feature fell outside the candidate's window. We recorded the retention of the correct structure, the fraction of candidates eliminated, the number retained and the rank of the correct structure among the candidates ordered by the standardised RT deviation $|t_{\mathrm{obs}}-g(s(x))|/\mathrm{scale}(x)$ ($n=20$ random calibrants, draws 0–4).

*External laboratories.* For the 37 reversed-phase NTS methods of the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison [@malm2025projection], multipliers were calibrated on all RepoRT systems (each scored by a model that excluded its own source group) and applied without change. Two protocols were used: $n\in\{5,10,20\}$ random calibrants, and each laboratory's calibration chemicals as calibrants with the suspects as analytes. For the second protocol, the replayed rule was "the NORMAN calibration chemicals measured on the system" (36 RepoRT systems contained at least ten of them).

## Metrics

For each system and draw (a 'laboratory') we computed the retention, i.e. the empirical coverage (fraction of test compounds whose RT lies in their window); the primary endpoint for laboratory-level control is the *failure rate*, the share of laboratories whose retention falls below $1-\varepsilon=0.90$ (target $\le\delta=0.10$). We also computed mean and median window width (min), and the Winkler interval score [@winkler1972decision; @gneiting2007strictly]
$$\mathrm{IS}_\alpha(l,u;t)=(u-l)+\frac{2}{\alpha}(l-t)\,\mathbb{1}\{t<l\}+\frac{2}{\alpha}(t-u)\,\mathbb{1}\{t>u\},$$
a proper score that rewards narrow windows and penalises misses. Because it does not by itself prevent an under-covering method from scoring well, widths are compared only between methods that meet the same guarantee. Candidate-filtering performance was evaluated with *isomeric decoys*: for each test compound, up to 30 RepoRT compounds with the same molecular formula but a different connectivity block were treated as incorrect candidates; a decoy was eliminated when the observed RT of the true compound fell outside the decoy's window (draws 0–4 at $n=20$).

## Statistical analysis

Failure rates are reported with 95 % confidence intervals from a cluster bootstrap over target systems (draws of one system resampled together; 2,000 resamples) and per held-out source group, because the 228 systems come from five source groups whose results are not independent. The unit of analysis is the target system; metrics were averaged over draws within each system. Mean coverage is reported with 95 % bootstrap confidence intervals over systems (10,000 resamples for the main analyses and 1,000–4,000 for the supplementary analyses) and, as a sensitivity analysis, with a cluster bootstrap over source folds. Coverage validity was assessed with the pre-specified one-sided bootstrap test of the null hypothesis that mean coverage is at least $1-\alpha$ (10,000 resamples of systems under the null; cluster bootstrap over source groups as sensitivity analysis). The proposed method was compared with each comparator by two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [@wilcoxon1945individual] on per-system interval scores, mean widths and absolute coverage gaps $|1-\alpha-\text{coverage}|$, with Holm correction within each metric family [@holm1979simple] and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation as effect size [@kerby2014simple]. Robustness experiments used 10 draws per system.

## Implementation

All computations ran on a 4-core CPU without GPU, with Python 3.13, RDKit 2026.09.2 [@rdkit], XGBoost 3.4.1 [@chen2016xgboost], LightGBM 4.7.0 [@ke2017lightgbm], scikit-learn 1.9.1 [@pedregosa2011scikit] and SciPy 1.18.1 [@virtanen2020scipy]. The analysis plan and its addenda were time-stamped in version control before the corresponding runs; every number, table and figure was generated by scripts from the raw outputs, without manual transcription. The code is available from the corresponding author on request.


# Results and discussion

## Average guarantees do not protect the individual laboratory

With 20 randomly chosen calibrants and a target retention of 90 %, the conventional windows retained the correct structure less often than intended on average. Mean retention over the 228 held-out systems was 0.822 for Gaussian-process projection intervals, 0.851 for P-spline prediction intervals, 0.885 for the Gaussian ±z·RMSE window and 0.705 for a fixed ±1 min window (Supplementary Table S29). Calibrant-based conformal windows reached the nominal level on average: 0.909 for jackknife+ and 0.911 with the meta-learned normalisation (Table 2).

This average, however, hides what a laboratory experiences. Each combination of a system and a calibrant draw is one laboratory. Among these, retention fell below 0.90 for 35 % of laboratories with jackknife+, 34 % with the normalised jackknife+ and 48 % with the Gaussian window. It fell below 0.85 for 18 %, 17 % and 28 % (Table 2, Fig. 2a). For one laboratory in twenty, a jackknife+ window kept fewer than 76 % of the correct structures. This is not a defect of jackknife+: its guarantee is marginal and was met. A marginal guarantee is simply the wrong promise for a laboratory, which has one calibrant set and cannot tell whether its own retention lies above or below the average. Calibrating a marginal target across systems did not change this either. XSC with the average target matched the nominal mean (0.902) but still failed 41 % of laboratories. More calibrants made it worse: at $n=40$ the windows were narrower, but jackknife+ failed 39 % of laboratories, because retention concentrates around the nominal value and about half of the laboratories fall just below it (Fig. 2b).

## Cross-system calibration controls retention in each laboratory

XSC with the laboratory-level target reduced the share of failing laboratories to 8.0 % (95 % CI 7.1 %–9.0 %), below the specified δ = 10 % (Table 2). Fewer than 2.9 % of laboratories fell below 0.85, and the 5th percentile of laboratory retention rose from 0.759 (jackknife+) to 0.878. The control held when entire source groups were unseen. For each held-out source, between 5 % and 11 % of laboratories failed, against 33 %–39 % with jackknife+ (Table S30). It also held across levels: over all nominal δ from 0.02 to 0.30 and ε ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.20}, the observed failure rate stayed at or below the nominal one (Fig. S3; e.g. 3.8 % observed at δ = 0.10, ε = 0.10). Multipliers from the exact one-replay-per-system rule of Section 2.6 gave practically the same failure rates (3.4 %). The guarantee is therefore conservative rather than exact under leave-source-out shift. This is the expected direction when the training systems are, if anything, more heterogeneous than the systems of one source group.

The price of the laboratory-level promise is width. The median window was 5.70 min wide, against 4.38 min for jackknife+ (41 % versus 33 % of the RT span). The cost should, however, be compared with constructions that keep the same promise. Jackknife+ windows given the same cross-system laboratory-level calibration (B8) also met the target (9.2 % failing) but were 7.01 min wide. The same holds for a calibrated ±c·RMSE rule (B9; 9.5 %, 6.81 min). The proposed windows were narrower than B8 for 92 % of systems (median width ratio 0.80) and narrower than B9 for 90 % (ratio 0.81). Here the meta-learned difficulty model earns its place. Within jackknife+ it barely changed efficiency, because jackknife+ re-estimates the window from 20 residuals in every laboratory. Within XSC it serves as a prior: through the shrinkage of $\hat\tau$ it makes the laboratory's estimated error scale far less variable. A less variable scale needs a smaller multiplier to protect the unlucky laboratories.

## Finite, guaranteed windows from three calibrants

Calibrant-only conformal windows are infinite below $n=1/\alpha-1=9$. XSC windows remained finite and controlled with three calibrants (7.8 % of laboratories failing; median width 16.1 min) and five calibrants (7.6 %; 10.8 min). The Gaussian window, the only conventional alternative at these numbers, failed 46 % and 49 % of laboratories (Fig. 2b). Here shrinkage is decisive. Without it, the multiplier needed to protect 90 % of laboratories with three calibrants made the windows 109 min wide (σ ≡ 1), because three residuals cannot estimate a laboratory's error scale. With 10 calibrants the proposed windows (7.30 min, 7.2 % failing) were narrower than lab-calibrated jackknife+ (10.32 min). Width decreased steadily with $n$, from 7.30 min at $n=10$ to 5.70 and 5.04 min at 20 and 40 calibrants (Fig. 2c). A laboratory can therefore trade measurement effort against filtering power with a known guarantee at each step.

## Unrepresentative calibrants

Laboratories rarely pick calibrants at random. When calibrants were spread over the RT range, chosen as the compounds measured most often in public data, or chosen as the most polar compounds, the calibrants were no longer exchangeable with the analytes, and the calibrant-only windows lost even their average guarantee (Fig. 3). With the 20 most commonly measured compounds, jackknife+ failed 73 % of laboratories and the Gaussian window 76 %. With the 20 most polar compounds, which cover only the early part of the chromatogram, they failed 82 % and 85 %. Replaying the selection rule on the public systems restored laboratory-level control: 2.2 % failing with common compounds, 8.3 % with RT-spanning calibrants and 11.4 % with polar ones. For polar calibrants the price was high: the windows were 18 min wide, because every late-eluting compound must be extrapolated. The method thus reports, instead of hiding, that such a calibrant set gives little filtering power. RT-spanning calibrants, by contrast, gave the narrowest guaranteed windows of all rules (5.15 min at $n=20$; 6.36 min at $n=10$). This practical result follows directly from the replay: the multiplier of any candidate calibrant-selection rule can be computed before a single calibrant is measured, so laboratories can compare calibrant designs on public data.

## Filtering real candidate lists

The candidate lists were all PubChemLite isomers of each test compound. They had a median of 15 candidates per feature, over 251,498 feature–laboratory pairs (Table 3). On these lists, the heuristic and calibrant-only windows eliminated more incorrect isomers: 30 % with ±z·RMSE and 26 % with jackknife+. They paid for it with the correct structure, of which they retained 88.9 % and 91.2 %. Counted per laboratory, 44 % and 33 % of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of correct structures.

All windows with the laboratory-level guarantee retained more than 96 % of correct structures. Only 6.6 % (proposed), 8.7 % (σ ≡ 1) and 7.3 % (lab-calibrated jackknife+) of laboratories fell below 90 %. Each eliminated about one incorrect isomer in eight (12.5 %, 12.8 % and 12.6 %). The narrower median width of the proposed windows (Table 2) therefore did not translate into more eliminated isomers. A plausible explanation, not tested directly, is that the meta-learned scale widens windows for compounds unlike the training data, and most PubChemLite isomers are such compounds. The scale would then move width to where the candidates are.

Halving the elimination rate of heuristic filters is the true cost of not discarding correct structures; heuristic windows hide this cost rather than avoid it. For ranking the retained candidates, the plain RT deviation was better than the σ-normalised one. The correct structure ranked first in 31 % of cases and among the first three in 55 %, against 19 % and 42 % with σ-normalised deviations. We therefore recommend guaranteed windows for filtering, followed by ranking by plain deviation, ahead of MS/MS-based scoring.

## Real-world case study: 37 laboratories of the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison

In the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison, 37 laboratories analysed the same calibration chemicals and suspects with their own reversed-phase NTS methods [@malm2025projection]. None of these laboratories, instruments or gradients was used for model development or calibration; the XSC multipliers came from RepoRT alone.

*Random calibrants.* With calibrants drawn at random from each laboratory's compounds, the laboratory-level control carried over to these external data. With 20 calibrants, 8.9 % of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of correct structures. The corresponding figures were 9.5 % with 10 calibrants and 9.7 % with 5. Jackknife+ failed 34 % of laboratories and the Gaussian window 49 % (Table S31).

*The laboratory's own calibration mix.* We then treated each laboratory as in practice. Its own calibration chemicals served as calibrants, its detected suspects were the analytes, and each suspect's candidate list contained all its PubChemLite isomers (median 9; 1,186 suspect–laboratory pairs). The fixed mix is the realistic case of non-exchangeable calibrants. The windows in current use retained the correct suspect structure in only 85 % (±z·RMSE), 90 % (±2·RMSE) and 86 % (jackknife+) of cases. Between 35 % and 59 % of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % (Table 4). These filters eliminated 16 %–21 % of the incorrect isomers, but at the price of silently discarding the correct structure for one suspect in seven to ten.

XSC replayed the rule "the NORMAN calibration chemicals measured on the system" on the 36 RepoRT systems that contained them. With σ ≡ 1, it retained 96.9 % of correct structures, and 8.1 % of laboratories fell below 90 %, within the specified δ. With the shrunk meta-learned scale, retention was 90.2 %, but 43 % of laboratories fell below 90 % (8 % below 85 %). The difficulty model therefore did not transfer to these methods. This is consistent with their incomplete metadata (no temperature, flow rate or dead time) and with the 36 replay systems available for this rule, against 263 for random calibrants. The recommended default for laboratories with incomplete metadata or unusual calibrant mixes is thus σ ≡ 1, whose guarantee does not depend on the difficulty model.

With the guarantee respected, RT removed only 4 % of the incorrect isomers in these laboratories. This sobering figure, rather than the 15–22 % of the heuristic filters, is the filtering power that the calibration mix of the NORMAN comparison supports for the individual laboratory. The larger eliminations reported for heuristic windows are, to a substantial degree, bought by discarding correct structures. The ranking of candidates by RT deviation was unaffected by the window: the correct structure ranked first among the retained candidates in 28 % of cases and among the first three in 54 %. Ranking by σ-normalised deviation was worse (10 % top-1), so the plain deviation should be used for ranking. RT therefore remains useful for prioritisation even where a guaranteed filter removes few candidates.

## Where the guarantee can fail

The laboratory-level guarantee rests on exchangeability of the laboratory with public systems, and three situations weaken it. First, a laboratory unlike all public systems — a stationary phase, mobile-phase pH or analyte class absent from repositories — is not covered. Restricting the calibration to the 60 public systems most similar in reported chromatographic metadata ("localized XSC", post hoc) gave failure rates similar to global calibration (4.2 % versus 4.9 %), so the current repository is diverse enough. As repositories grow, localization offers a route to narrower windows for typical laboratories. Second, the analytes of a laboratory are represented by the compounds measured on public systems, 73 % of which were known to the base model; for analytes outside its applicability domain the guarantee holds only to the extent that the difficulty model widens their windows (Section S5). Third, the calibrant RTs are assumed correct; a mis-assigned calibrant inflates $\hat\tau$ and widens the windows (conservative), but a drift between calibration and sample runs is not covered. These assumptions are explicit and testable, whereas the heuristic windows in use today carry the same assumptions implicitly and, as shown above, fail far more laboratories.

## Practical use

A laboratory needs only the structures and RTs of its calibrants, its routine chromatographic metadata and the candidate structures. It chooses the retention it requires ($1-\varepsilon$) and the risk it accepts ($\delta$), and reads off the multiplier calibrated for its calibrant number and selection rule. These multipliers are computed once from public data (Table S28); applying them costs a few milliseconds per candidate. Three recommendations follow from the results. (i) Report RT filters with a laboratory-level guarantee rather than a fixed or residual-based tolerance. (ii) Prefer calibrants spread over the RT range; five to ten such calibrants already give finite guaranteed windows. (iii) Before measuring, compare candidate calibrant mixes by replaying them on public data; a mix that yields very wide windows is uninformative for RT filtering, whatever its point accuracy.


# Conclusions

Conformal RT windows built from a laboratory's calibrants keep their nominal retention only on average. With 20 calibrants, about a third of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of the correct structures, and conventional windows did worse. Cross-system conformal calibration replaces this marginal promise with one that concerns the laboratory itself. The laboratory's calibrants place and scale the window, and the public record of hundreds of chromatographic systems calibrates its width for exactly the laboratory's protocol. Retention of at least 90 % was then achieved in at least 90 % of laboratories (8.0 % failing), on systems from source groups never seen in training and in 37 external NTS laboratories. With their own calibration mixes, the variant without the meta-learned scale kept the guarantee. The method gives finite, guaranteed windows from three calibrants and for calibrant mixes that do not resemble the analytes. A meta-learned difficulty model, which added little within calibrant-only conformal windows, became useful as a prior that stabilises a laboratory's error scale and narrows guaranteed windows on systems resembling the public data. It did not transfer to external methods with incomplete metadata, where σ ≡ 1 is the safe default. The real-world case study also puts current practice in perspective. In the NORMAN laboratories, heuristic RT filters removed more isomers only by discarding the correct structure for one suspect in seven to ten, and with the guarantee respected their calibration mix supported little filtering, although RT remained useful for ranking. Because the multiplier for any calibrant design can be computed from public data before measurement, the approach also tells a laboratory which calibrants are worth measuring. The guarantee requires the laboratory and its analytes to resemble public systems and their compounds. Growing repositories will extend its reach, and the same cross-system construction applies to collision cross sections and other system-dependent evidence. Combining such laboratory-level guarantees across RT, collision cross section and MS/MS evidence is the natural next step towards identification with stated error rates.
