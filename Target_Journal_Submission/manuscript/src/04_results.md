# Results and discussion

## Average guarantees do not protect the individual laboratory

With 20 randomly chosen calibrants and a target retention of 90 %, most conventional windows retained the correct structure less often than intended on average. Mean retention over the {{n_target_systems}} held-out systems was {{main.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for Gaussian-process projection intervals, {{main.B2_pspline_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for P-spline prediction intervals, {{x.random.20.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian ±z·RMSE window and {{fw.FIX_1min.n20.coverage_mean|.3f}} for a fixed ±1 min window (Table 2; Supplementary Tables S6 and S29). Wider heuristic windows over-covered on average: ±3·RMSE reached {{x.random.20.RMSE_x3.coverage_mean|.3f}} and the GP projection with a meta-learned prior (B7) {{x.random.20.B7_gp_meta.coverage_mean|.3f}}. Calibrant-based conformal windows reached the nominal level on average: {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+ and {{x.random.20.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} with the meta-learned normalisation (Table 2).

This average, however, hides what a laboratory experiences. Each combination of a system and a calibrant draw is one laboratory. Among these, retention fell below 0.90 for {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories with jackknife+, {{x.random.20.P.fail|pct0}} with the normalised jackknife+ and {{x.random.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} with the Gaussian window. It fell below 0.85 for {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail_085|pct0}}, {{x.random.20.P.fail_085|pct0}} and {{x.random.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail_085|pct0}} (Table 2, Fig. 2a). For one laboratory in twenty, a jackknife+ window kept fewer than {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.q05|pct0}} of the correct structures. This is not a defect of jackknife+: its guarantee is marginal and was met. A marginal guarantee is simply the wrong promise for a laboratory, which has one calibrant set and cannot tell whether its own retention lies above or below the average. Calibrating a marginal target across systems did not change this either. XSC with the average target matched the nominal mean ({{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_avg.coverage_mean|.3f}}) but still failed {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_avg.fail|pct0}} of laboratories. More calibrants made it worse: at $n=40$ the windows were narrower, but jackknife+ failed {{x.random.40.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories, because retention concentrates around the nominal value and many laboratories fall just below it (Fig. 2b).

## Cross-system calibration controls retention in each laboratory

XSC with the laboratory-level target reduced the share of failing laboratories to {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} (95 % CI {{x.ci.XSC_sigs_lab.lo|pct}}–{{x.ci.XSC_sigs_lab.hi|pct}}), below the specified δ = 10 % (Table 2). Only {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail_085|pct}} of laboratories fell below 0.85, and the 5th percentile of laboratory retention rose from {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.q05|.3f}} (jackknife+) to {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.q05|.3f}}. Control was close to the target when entire source groups were unseen. For each held-out source, between {{x.src.min_fail_proposed|pct}} and {{x.src.max_fail_proposed|pct}} of laboratories failed, against {{x.src.min_fail_jp|pct0}}–{{x.src.max_fail_jp|pct0}} with jackknife+ (Table S30). The target was marginally exceeded for one of the five source groups. It also held across levels: over all nominal δ from 0.02 to 0.30 and ε ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.20}, the observed failure rate stayed at or below the nominal one (Fig. S3; e.g. {{xo.cal.random.0.10.0.10|pct}} observed at δ = 0.10, ε = 0.10). Multipliers from the exact one-replay-per-system rule of Section 2.6 gave practically the same failure rates ({{xo.calx.random.0.10.0.10|pct}}). The guarantee is therefore conservative rather than exact under leave-source-out shift. This is the expected direction for two reasons. The replay systems are scored by base models trained on one source group less than the model used for the target, so their errors are slightly larger. And the training systems are, if anything, more heterogeneous than the systems of one source group.

The price of the laboratory-level promise is width. The median window was {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min wide, against {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.width_median|.2f}} min for jackknife+ ({{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_rel_median|pct0}} versus {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.width_rel_median|pct0}} of the RT span). The cost should, however, be compared with constructions that keep the same promise. Jackknife+ windows given the same cross-system laboratory-level calibration (B8) also met the target ({{x.random.20.JP_lab.fail|pct}} failing) but were {{x.random.20.JP_lab.width_median|.2f}} min wide. The same holds for a calibrated ±c·RMSE rule (B9; {{x.random.20.XSC_flat_lab.fail|pct}}, {{x.random.20.XSC_flat_lab.width_median|.2f}} min). The proposed windows were narrower than B8 for {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_JP_lab.share_narrower|pct0}} of systems (median width ratio {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_JP_lab.median|.2f}}) and narrower than B9 for {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_XSC_flat_lab.share_narrower|pct0}} (ratio {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_XSC_flat_lab.median|.2f}}). Two heuristics also met the target with 20 random calibrants: ±3·RMSE ({{x.random.20.RMSE_x3.fail|pct}} failing; {{x.random.20.RMSE_x3.width_median|.2f}} min) and the meta-learned GP projection B7 ({{x.random.20.B7_gp_meta.fail|pct}}; {{x.random.20.B7_gp_meta.width_median|.2f}} min). Both were wider than the proposed windows, and neither is calibrated for anything but this protocol. The cross-system multiplier explains why ±3·RMSE works here: XSC with σ ≡ 1 and no shrinkage is a ±c·RMSE rule, and for 20 random calibrants it selected c ≈ 3. For five calibrants it selected c ≈ 6.5, for three c ≈ 34, and for non-random calibrant mixes different values again (Table S28). XSC thus supplies, for each protocol, the multiplier that the folklore ±3·RMSE guesses for one. Here the meta-learned difficulty model earns its place. Within jackknife+ it barely changed efficiency, because jackknife+ re-estimates the window from 20 residuals in every laboratory. Within XSC it serves as a prior: through the shrinkage of $\hat\tau$ it makes the laboratory's estimated error scale far less variable. A less variable scale needs a smaller multiplier to protect the unlucky laboratories.

## Finite, guaranteed windows from three calibrants

Calibrant-only conformal windows are infinite below $n=1/\alpha-1=9$. XSC windows remained finite and controlled with three calibrants ({{x.random.3.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} of laboratories failing; median width {{x.random.3.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.1f}} min) and five calibrants ({{x.random.5.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}}; {{x.random.5.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.1f}} min). The Gaussian window, the only conventional alternative at these numbers, failed {{x.random.3.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} and {{x.random.5.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} of laboratories (Fig. 2b). Here shrinkage is decisive. Without it, the multiplier needed to protect 90 % of laboratories with three calibrants made the windows {{x.random.3.XSC_flat_lab.width_median|.0f}} min wide (σ ≡ 1), because three residuals cannot estimate a laboratory's error scale. With 10 calibrants the proposed windows ({{x.random.10.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min, {{x.random.10.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} failing) were narrower than lab-calibrated jackknife+ ({{x.random.10.JP_lab.width_median|.2f}} min). Width decreased steadily with $n$, from {{x.random.10.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at $n=10$ to {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} and {{x.random.40.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at 20 and 40 calibrants (Fig. 2c). A laboratory can therefore trade measurement effort against filtering power with a known guarantee at each step.

## Unrepresentative calibrants

Laboratories rarely pick calibrants at random. When calibrants were spread over the RT range, chosen as the compounds measured most often in public data, or chosen as the most polar compounds, the calibrants were no longer exchangeable with the analytes, and, except for RT-spanning calibrants, the calibrant-only windows lost even their average guarantee (Fig. 3). With the 20 most commonly measured compounds, jackknife+ failed {{x.common.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories and the Gaussian window {{x.common.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}}. With the 20 most polar compounds, which cover only the early part of the chromatogram, they failed {{x.polar.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} and {{x.polar.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}}. Replaying the selection rule on the public systems restored laboratory-level control: {{x.common.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} of laboratories failed with common compounds and {{x.span.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with RT-spanning calibrants. With polar calibrants {{x.polar.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} failed, slightly above δ. For polar calibrants the price was high: the windows were {{x.polar.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.0f}} min wide, because every late-eluting compound must be extrapolated. The method thus reports, instead of hiding, that such a calibrant set gives little filtering power. RT-spanning calibrants, by contrast, gave the narrowest guaranteed windows of all rules ({{x.span.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at $n=20$; {{x.span.10.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at $n=10$). This practical result follows directly from the replay: the multiplier of any candidate calibrant-selection rule can be computed before a single calibrant is measured, so laboratories can compare calibrant designs on public data.

## Filtering real candidate lists

The candidate lists were all PubChemLite isomers of each test compound. They had a median of {{xc.median_list|.0f}} candidates per feature, over {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.features|int}} feature–laboratory pairs (Table 3). On these lists, the heuristic and calibrant-only windows eliminated more incorrect isomers: {{xc.B1_gauss_rmse.elim|pct0}} with ±z·RMSE and {{xc.B5_jackknife_plus.elim|pct0}} with jackknife+. They paid for it with the correct structure, of which they retained {{xc.B1_gauss_rmse.kept|pct}} and {{xc.B5_jackknife_plus.kept|pct}}. Counted per laboratory, {{xc.labfail.B1_gauss_rmse|pct0}} and {{xc.labfail.B5_jackknife_plus|pct0}} of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of correct structures.

All windows with the laboratory-level guarantee retained more than 96 % of correct structures. Only {{xc.labfail.XSC_sigs_lab|pct}} (proposed), {{xc.labfail.XSC_flat_lab|pct}} (σ ≡ 1) and {{xc.labfail.JP_lab|pct}} (lab-calibrated jackknife+) of laboratories fell below 90 %. Each eliminated about one incorrect isomer in eight ({{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.elim|pct}}, {{xc.XSC_flat_lab.elim|pct}} and {{xc.JP_lab.elim|pct}}). The narrower median width of the proposed windows (Table 2) therefore did not translate into more eliminated isomers. A plausible explanation, not tested directly, is that the meta-learned scale widens windows for compounds unlike the training data, and most PubChemLite isomers are such compounds. The scale would then move width to where the candidates are.

Halving the elimination rate of heuristic filters is the true cost of not discarding correct structures; heuristic windows hide this cost rather than avoid it. For ranking the retained candidates, the plain RT deviation was better than the σ-normalised one. The correct structure ranked first in {{xc.XSC_flat_lab.top1|pct0}} of cases and among the first three in {{xc.XSC_flat_lab.top3|pct0}}, against {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.top1|pct0}} and {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.top3|pct0}} with σ-normalised deviations. We therefore recommend guaranteed windows for filtering, followed by ranking by plain deviation, ahead of MS/MS-based scoring.

## Retrospective case study: 37 laboratories of the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison

In the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison, 37 laboratories analysed the same calibration chemicals and suspects with their own reversed-phase NTS methods [@malm2025projection]. None of these laboratories, instruments or gradients was used for model development or calibration; the XSC multipliers came from RepoRT alone.

*Random calibrants.* With calibrants drawn at random from each laboratory's compounds, the laboratory-level control carried over to these external data. With 20 calibrants, {{xn.random_all.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of correct structures. The corresponding figures were {{xn.random_all.10.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with 10 calibrants and {{xn.random_all.5.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with 5. Jackknife+ failed {{xn.random_all.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories and the Gaussian window {{xn.random_all.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} (Table S31).

*The laboratory's own calibration mix.* We then treated each laboratory as in practice. Its own calibration chemicals served as calibrants, its detected suspects were the analytes, and each suspect's candidate list contained all its PubChemLite isomers (median {{xk.median_list|.0f}}; {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.n|int}} suspect–laboratory pairs). The fixed mix is the realistic case of non-exchangeable calibrants. The windows in current use retained the correct suspect structure in only {{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.kept|pct0}} (±z·RMSE), {{xk.RMSE_x2.kept|pct0}} (±2·RMSE) and {{xk.B5_jackknife_plus.kept|pct0}} (jackknife+) of cases. Between {{xk.RMSE_x2.labfail|pct0}} and {{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.labfail|pct0}} of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % (Table 4). These filters eliminated {{xk.RMSE_x2.elim|pct0}}–{{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.elim|pct0}} of the incorrect isomers, but at the price of silently discarding the correct structure for {{xk.RMSE_x2.miss|pct0}}–{{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.miss|pct0}} of the suspects.

XSC replayed the rule "the NORMAN calibration chemicals measured on the system" on the {{xn.S_mix}} RepoRT systems that contained them. With σ ≡ 1, it retained {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.kept|pct}} of correct structures, and {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.labfail|pct}} of laboratories fell below 90 % (3 of 37; 95 % CI {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.labfail_lo|pct0}}–{{xk.XSC_flat_lab.labfail_hi|pct0}}), consistent with δ = 10 %. With the shrunk meta-learned scale, retention was {{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.kept|pct}}, but {{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.labfail|pct0}} of laboratories fell below 90 % ({{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.labfail85|pct0}} below 85 %). The NORMAN methods do not report temperature, flow rate or dead time. Replaying the protocol with these descriptors blanked on the RepoRT systems (post hoc) left the failure unchanged ({{xkm.XSC_sigs_lab.labfail|pct0}} of laboratories below 90 %). Missing metadata are therefore not the cause. The more likely cause is a mismatch of analyte populations. In the replay, the analytes of a RepoRT system are all its compounds other than the mix chemicals, whereas in the NORMAN laboratories they are suspects selected for the comparison. The difficulty model judges these two populations differently relative to the calibration chemicals, and only 36 replay systems were available for this rule, against 263 for random calibrants. With σ ≡ 1, the scale is estimated from the mix's own residuals and the guarantee does not depend on the difficulty model. We therefore recommend σ ≡ 1 for fixed calibration mixes whose analytes differ from the compounds measured on public systems, and the shrunk scale when the calibrants are chosen by a rule that can be replayed on public data.

With the guarantee respected, RT removed only {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.elim|pct0}} of the incorrect isomers in these laboratories. This sobering figure, rather than the {{xk.RMSE_x2.elim|pct0}}–{{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.elim|pct0}} of the heuristic filters, is the filtering power that the calibration mix of the NORMAN comparison supports for the individual laboratory. The larger eliminations reported for heuristic windows are, to a substantial degree, bought by discarding correct structures. Because the guaranteed window rarely removes the correct structure, it also gave the most correct identifications when candidates were ranked by RT deviation. The correct structure was retained and ranked first in {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.top1|pct}} of cases, against {{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.top1|pct}} (±z·RMSE), {{xk.B5_jackknife_plus.top1|pct}} (jackknife+) and {{xk.RMSE_x2.top1|pct}} (±2·RMSE). It was among the first three in {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.top3|pct0}} of cases. Ranking by σ-normalised deviation was worse ({{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.top1|pct0}} top-1), so the plain deviation should be used for ranking. RT therefore remains useful for prioritisation even where a guaranteed filter removes few candidates.

## Where the guarantee can fail

The laboratory-level guarantee rests on exchangeability of the laboratory with public systems, and three situations weaken it. First, a laboratory unlike all public systems — a stationary phase, mobile-phase pH or analyte class absent from repositories — is not covered. Restricting the calibration to the 60 public systems most similar in reported chromatographic metadata ("localized XSC", post hoc) gave failure rates similar to global calibration ({{xo.loc.random.20.XSC_local_K60.fail|pct}} versus {{xo.loc.random.20.XSC_lab.fail|pct}}), although this check is limited to one held-out source group (Section S8). As repositories grow, localization offers a route to narrower windows for typical laboratories. Second, the analytes of a laboratory are represented by the compounds measured on public systems, {{share_seen_test}} of which were known to the base model; for analytes outside its applicability domain the guarantee holds only to the extent that the difficulty model widens their windows (Section S5). Third, the calibrant RTs are assumed correct; a mis-assigned calibrant inflates $\hat\tau$ and widens the windows (conservative), but a drift between calibration and sample runs is not covered. These assumptions are explicit and testable, whereas the heuristic windows in use today carry the same assumptions implicitly and, as shown above, fail far more laboratories.

## Practical use

For fixed calibration mixes, σ ≡ 1 is the robust choice (Section 4.6). A laboratory needs only the structures and RTs of its calibrants, its routine chromatographic metadata and the candidate structures. It chooses the retention it requires ($1-\varepsilon$) and the risk it accepts ($\delta$), and reads off the multiplier calibrated for its calibrant number and selection rule. These multipliers are computed once from public data (Table S28); applying them costs a few milliseconds per candidate. Three recommendations follow from the results. (i) Report RT filters with a laboratory-level guarantee rather than a fixed or residual-based tolerance. (ii) Prefer calibrants spread over the RT range; five to ten such calibrants already give finite guaranteed windows. (iii) Before measuring, compare candidate calibrant mixes by replaying them on public data; a mix that yields very wide windows is uninformative for RT filtering, whatever its point accuracy.
