# Results and discussion

## Average guarantees do not protect the individual laboratory

With 20 randomly chosen calibrants and a target retention of 90 %, the conventional windows retained the correct structure less often than intended on average. Mean retention over the {{n_target_systems}} held-out systems was {{main.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for Gaussian-process projection intervals, {{main.B2_pspline_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for P-spline prediction intervals, {{x.random.20.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian ±z·RMSE window and {{fw.FIX_1min.n20.coverage_mean|.3f}} for a fixed ±1 min window (Supplementary Table S29). Calibrant-based conformal windows reached the nominal level on average: {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+ and {{x.random.20.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} with the meta-learned normalisation (Table 2).

This average, however, hides what a laboratory experiences. Each combination of a system and a calibrant draw is one laboratory. Among these, retention fell below 0.90 for {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories with jackknife+, {{x.random.20.P.fail|pct0}} with the normalised jackknife+ and {{x.random.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} with the Gaussian window. It fell below 0.85 for {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail_085|pct0}}, {{x.random.20.P.fail_085|pct0}} and {{x.random.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail_085|pct0}} (Table 2, Fig. 2a). For one laboratory in twenty, a jackknife+ window kept fewer than {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.q05|pct0}} of the correct structures. This is not a defect of jackknife+: its guarantee is marginal and was met. A marginal guarantee is simply the wrong promise for a laboratory, which has one calibrant set and cannot tell whether its own retention lies above or below the average. Calibrating a marginal target across systems did not change this either. XSC with the average target matched the nominal mean ({{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_avg.coverage_mean|.3f}}) but still failed {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_avg.fail|pct0}} of laboratories. More calibrants made it worse: at $n=40$ the windows were narrower, but jackknife+ failed {{x.random.40.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories, because retention concentrates around the nominal value and about half of the laboratories fall just below it (Fig. 2b).

## Cross-system calibration controls retention in each laboratory

XSC with the laboratory-level target reduced the share of failing laboratories to {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} (95 % CI {{x.ci.XSC_sigs_lab.lo|pct}}–{{x.ci.XSC_sigs_lab.hi|pct}}), below the specified δ = 10 % (Table 2). Fewer than {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail_085|pct}} of laboratories fell below 0.85, and the 5th percentile of laboratory retention rose from {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.q05|.3f}} (jackknife+) to {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.q05|.3f}}. The control held when entire source groups were unseen. For each held-out source, between {{x.src.min_fail_proposed|pct0}} and {{x.src.max_fail_proposed|pct0}} of laboratories failed, against {{x.src.min_fail_jp|pct0}}–{{x.src.max_fail_jp|pct0}} with jackknife+ (Table S30). It also held across levels: over all nominal δ from 0.02 to 0.30 and ε ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.20}, the observed failure rate stayed at or below the nominal one (Fig. S3; e.g. {{xo.cal.random.0.10.0.10|pct}} observed at δ = 0.10, ε = 0.10). Multipliers from the exact one-replay-per-system rule of Section 2.6 gave practically the same failure rates ({{xo.calx.random.0.10.0.10|pct}}). The guarantee is therefore conservative rather than exact under leave-source-out shift. This is the expected direction when the training systems are, if anything, more heterogeneous than the systems of one source group.

The price of the laboratory-level promise is width. The median window was {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min wide, against {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.width_median|.2f}} min for jackknife+ ({{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_rel_median|pct0}} versus {{x.random.20.B5_jackknife_plus.width_rel_median|pct0}} of the RT span). The cost should, however, be compared with constructions that keep the same promise. Jackknife+ windows given the same cross-system laboratory-level calibration (B8) also met the target ({{x.random.20.JP_lab.fail|pct}} failing) but were {{x.random.20.JP_lab.width_median|.2f}} min wide. The same holds for a calibrated ±c·RMSE rule (B9; {{x.random.20.XSC_flat_lab.fail|pct}}, {{x.random.20.XSC_flat_lab.width_median|.2f}} min). The proposed windows were narrower than B8 for {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_JP_lab.share_narrower|pct0}} of systems (median width ratio {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_JP_lab.median|.2f}}) and narrower than B9 for {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_XSC_flat_lab.share_narrower|pct0}} (ratio {{x.ratio_XSC_sigs_lab_vs_XSC_flat_lab.median|.2f}}). Here the meta-learned difficulty model earns its place. Within jackknife+ it barely changed efficiency, because jackknife+ re-estimates the window from 20 residuals in every laboratory. Within XSC it serves as a prior: through the shrinkage of $\hat\tau$ it makes the laboratory's estimated error scale far less variable. A less variable scale needs a smaller multiplier to protect the unlucky laboratories.

## Finite, guaranteed windows from three calibrants

Calibrant-only conformal windows are infinite below $n=1/\alpha-1=9$. XSC windows remained finite and controlled with three calibrants ({{x.random.3.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} of laboratories failing; median width {{x.random.3.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.1f}} min) and five calibrants ({{x.random.5.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}}; {{x.random.5.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.1f}} min). The Gaussian window, the only conventional alternative at these numbers, failed {{x.random.3.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} and {{x.random.5.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} of laboratories (Fig. 2b). Here shrinkage is decisive. Without it, the multiplier needed to protect 90 % of laboratories with three calibrants made the windows {{x.random.3.XSC_flat_lab.width_median|.0f}} min wide (σ ≡ 1), because three residuals cannot estimate a laboratory's error scale. With 10 calibrants the proposed windows ({{x.random.10.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min, {{x.random.10.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} failing) were narrower than lab-calibrated jackknife+ ({{x.random.10.JP_lab.width_median|.2f}} min). Width decreased steadily with $n$, from {{x.random.10.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at $n=10$ to {{x.random.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} and {{x.random.40.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at 20 and 40 calibrants (Fig. 2c). A laboratory can therefore trade measurement effort against filtering power with a known guarantee at each step.

## Unrepresentative calibrants

Laboratories rarely pick calibrants at random. When calibrants were spread over the RT range, chosen as the compounds measured most often in public data, or chosen as the most polar compounds, the calibrants were no longer exchangeable with the analytes, and the calibrant-only windows lost even their average guarantee (Fig. 3). With the 20 most commonly measured compounds, jackknife+ failed {{x.common.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories and the Gaussian window {{x.common.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}}. With the 20 most polar compounds, which cover only the early part of the chromatogram, they failed {{x.polar.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} and {{x.polar.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}}. Replaying the selection rule on the public systems restored laboratory-level control: {{x.common.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} failing with common compounds, {{x.span.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with RT-spanning calibrants and {{x.polar.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with polar ones. For polar calibrants the price was high: the windows were {{x.polar.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.0f}} min wide, because every late-eluting compound must be extrapolated. The method thus reports, instead of hiding, that such a calibrant set gives little filtering power. RT-spanning calibrants, by contrast, gave the narrowest guaranteed windows of all rules ({{x.span.20.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at $n=20$; {{x.span.10.XSC_sigs_lab.width_median|.2f}} min at $n=10$). This practical result follows directly from the replay: the multiplier of any candidate calibrant-selection rule can be computed before a single calibrant is measured, so laboratories can compare calibrant designs on public data.

## Filtering real candidate lists

On PubChemLite candidate lists (median {{xc.median_list|.0f}} isomers per feature; {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.features|int}} feature–laboratory pairs), the proposed windows retained {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.kept|pct}} of the correct structures and eliminated {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.elim|pct0}} of the incorrect isomers (Table 3). Jackknife+ eliminated more ({{xc.B5_jackknife_plus.elim|pct0}}) but retained fewer correct structures ({{xc.B5_jackknife_plus.kept|pct}}), and the Gaussian window retained {{xc.B1_gauss_rmse.kept|pct}}. Among windows that keep the laboratory-level promise, the proposed windows eliminated the most candidates: {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.elim|pct0}}, against {{xc.JP_lab.elim|pct0}} for lab-calibrated jackknife+ and {{xc.XSC_flat_lab.elim|pct0}} for the calibrated ±c·RMSE rule. The correct structure was ranked first among the retained candidates in {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.top1|pct0}} of cases and among the first three in {{xc.XSC_sigs_lab.top3|pct0}}. Retention time alone thus removes a sizeable share of isomeric candidates without silently discarding the answer. Its main value, however, is as a guaranteed pre-filter before MS/MS-based scoring, which cannot recover a correct structure that the filter has removed.

## Real-world case study: 37 laboratories of the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison

In the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison, 37 laboratories analysed the same calibration chemicals and suspects with their own reversed-phase NTS methods [@malm2025projection]. None of these laboratories, instruments or gradients was used for model development or calibration; the XSC multipliers came from RepoRT alone.

*Random calibrants.* With calibrants drawn at random from each laboratory's compounds, the laboratory-level control carried over to these external data. With 20 calibrants, {{xn.random_all.20.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % of correct structures. The corresponding figures were {{xn.random_all.10.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with 10 calibrants and {{xn.random_all.5.XSC_sigs_lab.fail|pct}} with 5. Jackknife+ failed {{xn.random_all.20.B5_jackknife_plus.fail|pct0}} of laboratories and the Gaussian window {{xn.random_all.20.B1_gauss_rmse.fail|pct0}} (Table S31).

*The laboratory's own calibration mix.* We then treated each laboratory as in practice. Its own calibration chemicals served as calibrants, its detected suspects were the analytes, and each suspect's candidate list contained all its PubChemLite isomers (median {{xk.median_list|.0f}}; {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.n|int}} suspect–laboratory pairs). The fixed mix is the realistic case of non-exchangeable calibrants. The windows in current use retained the correct suspect structure in only {{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.kept|pct0}} (±z·RMSE), {{xk.RMSE_x2.kept|pct0}} (±2·RMSE) and {{xk.B5_jackknife_plus.kept|pct0}} (jackknife+) of cases. Between {{xk.RMSE_x2.labfail|pct0}} and {{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.labfail|pct0}} of laboratories retained fewer than 90 % (Table 4). These filters eliminated {{xk.RMSE_x2.elim|pct0}}–{{xk.B1_gauss_rmse.elim|pct0}} of the incorrect isomers, but at the price of silently discarding the correct structure for one suspect in seven to ten.

XSC replayed the rule "the NORMAN calibration chemicals measured on the system" on the {{xn.S_mix}} RepoRT systems that contained them. With σ ≡ 1, it retained {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.kept|pct}} of correct structures, and {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.labfail|pct}} of laboratories fell below 90 %, within the specified δ. With the shrunk meta-learned scale, retention was {{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.kept|pct}}, but {{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.labfail|pct0}} of laboratories fell below 90 % ({{xn.fixed_mix.lab_mix.XSC_sigs_lab.fail085|pct0}} below 85 %). The difficulty model therefore did not transfer to these methods. This is consistent with their incomplete metadata (no temperature, flow rate or dead time) and with the 36 replay systems available for this rule, against 263 for random calibrants. The recommended default for laboratories with incomplete metadata or unusual calibrant mixes is thus σ ≡ 1, whose guarantee does not depend on the difficulty model.

With the guarantee respected, RT removed only {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.elim|pct0}} of the incorrect isomers in these laboratories. This sobering figure, rather than the 15–22 % of the heuristic filters, is the filtering power that the calibration mix of the NORMAN comparison supports for the individual laboratory. The larger eliminations reported for heuristic windows are, to a substantial degree, bought by discarding correct structures. The ranking of candidates by RT deviation was unaffected by the window: the correct structure ranked first among the retained candidates in {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.top1|pct0}} of cases and among the first three in {{xk.XSC_flat_lab.top3|pct0}}. Ranking by σ-normalised deviation was worse ({{xk.XSC_sigs_lab.top1|pct0}} top-1), so the plain deviation should be used for ranking. RT therefore remains useful for prioritisation even where a guaranteed filter removes few candidates.

## Where the guarantee can fail

The laboratory-level guarantee rests on exchangeability of the laboratory with public systems, and three situations weaken it. First, a laboratory unlike all public systems — a stationary phase, mobile-phase pH or analyte class absent from repositories — is not covered. Restricting the calibration to the 60 public systems most similar in reported chromatographic metadata ("localized XSC", post hoc) gave failure rates similar to global calibration ({{xo.loc.random.20.XSC_local_K60.fail|pct}} versus {{xo.loc.random.20.XSC_lab.fail|pct}}), so the current repository is diverse enough. As repositories grow, localization offers a route to narrower windows for typical laboratories. Second, the analytes of a laboratory are represented by the compounds measured on public systems, {{share_seen_test}} of which were known to the base model; for analytes outside its applicability domain the guarantee holds only to the extent that the difficulty model widens their windows (Section S5). Third, the calibrant RTs are assumed correct; a mis-assigned calibrant inflates $\hat\tau$ and widens the windows (conservative), but a drift between calibration and sample runs is not covered. These assumptions are explicit and testable, whereas the heuristic windows in use today carry the same assumptions implicitly and, as shown above, fail far more laboratories.

## Practical use

A laboratory needs only the structures and RTs of its calibrants, its routine chromatographic metadata and the candidate structures. It chooses the retention it requires ($1-\varepsilon$) and the risk it accepts ($\delta$), and reads off the multiplier calibrated for its calibrant number and selection rule. These multipliers are computed once from public data (Table S28); applying them costs a few milliseconds per candidate. Three recommendations follow from the results. (i) Report RT filters with a laboratory-level guarantee rather than a fixed or residual-based tolerance. (ii) Prefer calibrants spread over the RT range; five to ten such calibrants already give finite guaranteed windows. (iii) Before measuring, compare candidate calibrant mixes by replaying them on public data; a mix that yields very wide windows is uninformative for RT filtering, whatever its point accuracy.
