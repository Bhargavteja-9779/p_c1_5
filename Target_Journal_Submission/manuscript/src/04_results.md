# Results and discussion

## Coverage of current retention-time windows on new systems

With $n=20$ randomly drawn calibrants and a nominal 90 % window, the parametric windows retained the correct structure less often than intended (Table 2, Fig. 2a). Mean coverage over the {{n_target_systems}} target systems was {{main.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for Gaussian-process projection intervals, {{main.B2_pspline_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for P-spline prediction intervals and {{main.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian ±z·RMSE window, and coverage fell below 0.85 in {{main.B3_gp_PI.share_severe_under|pct0}}, {{main.B2_pspline_PI.share_severe_under|pct0}} and {{main.B1_gauss_rmse.share_severe_under|pct0}} of the systems, respectively. The deficit grew as calibrants became scarcer: at $n=10$, mean coverage was {{s1.B3_gp_PI.n10.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}} (GP), {{s1.B2_pspline_PI.n10.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}} (P-spline) and {{s1.B1_gauss_rmse.n10.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}} (Gaussian) (Fig. 3a). In contrast, every conformal construction reached the nominal level on average: {{main.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+, {{main.B4_split_conformal.coverage_mean|.3f}} for split conformal prediction and {{main.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} (95 % CI {{main.P.coverage_ci_lo|.3f}}–{{main.P.coverage_ci_hi|.3f}}) for the proposed windows, and no target system had a mean coverage below 0.85 with the proposed windows. The conformal windows tracked the nominal level at every $\alpha$ (at $n=20$: {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}} for $\alpha=0.05$ and {{s1.P.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}} for $\alpha=0.20$), whereas the parametric windows were miscalibrated by $\alpha$-dependent amounts, and the Gaussian window even in an $\alpha$-dependent direction (Fig. S2, Table S2): at $\alpha=0.05$ all three under-covered (Gaussian {{s1.B1_gauss_rmse.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}, P-spline {{s1.B2_pspline_PI.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}, GP {{s1.B3_gp_PI.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}), while at $\alpha=0.20$ the Gaussian window over-covered ({{s1.B1_gauss_rmse.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}}) and the GP and P-spline intervals still under-covered ({{s1.B3_gp_PI.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{s1.B2_pspline_PI.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}}). The pattern of the Gaussian window is consistent with heavy-tailed or heteroscedastic transfer residuals [@matyushin2024uncertainty], for which a normal quantile is too wide near the centre and too narrow in the tails. The pre-specified one-sided test found no evidence of under-coverage for any conformal construction ($p$ = {{valp.P.n20.a0.10|.2f}} for the proposed windows), whereas it rejected adequate coverage for the Gaussian, P-spline and GP windows (all $p$ < 0.001; Table S3). Coverage of the proposed and jackknife+ windows was at or above the nominal level in each of the five source folds ({{foldcov.B5_jackknife_plus.min|.3f}}–{{foldcov.P.max|.3f}}; split conformal {{foldcov.B4_split_conformal.min|.3f}}–{{foldcov.B4_split_conformal.max|.3f}}; Table S7), and a cluster bootstrap over source folds gave practically identical confidence intervals (Table S3). These results support the pre-specified hypotheses that windows derived from parametric error models transfer their optimism to new systems (H1), whereas calibrant-based conformal windows keep the promised error rate on average without any target-system training data (H2).

Fixed tolerances, which are still widely used, fared worse (Table 2, Table S6; evaluated on the same calibrant draws, post hoc). With 20 calibrants, windows of ±0.25, ±0.5, ±1 and ±2 min retained the correct structure in {{fw.FIX_0.25min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}}, {{fw.FIX_0.5min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}}, {{fw.FIX_1min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}} and {{fw.FIX_2min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}} of cases on average, and relative windows of ±5 % and ±10 % of the predicted RT in {{fw.FIX_5pct.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}} and {{fw.FIX_10pct.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}}. A fixed window cannot adapt to systems whose RT errors differ by an order of magnitude: even ±2 min kept fewer than 85 % of the correct structures in {{fw.FIX_2min.n20.share_severe_under|pct0}} of the systems. Residual-based windows of ±2·RMSE and ±3·RMSE, often read as approximately 95 % and 99.7 % intervals, delivered {{fw.RMSE_x2.n20.coverage_mean|pct}} and {{fw.RMSE_x3.n20.coverage_mean|pct}} at $n=20$ and {{fw.RMSE_x2.n10.coverage_mean|pct}} for ±2·RMSE at $n=10$. In terms of the mean interval score, the proposed windows were better than every fixed tolerance at every calibrant number and better than the ±2·RMSE and ±3·RMSE windows except at $n=15$ (±2·RMSE {{fw.RMSE_x2.n15.winkler_mean|.2f}} versus {{s1.P.n15.a0.10.winkler_mean|.2f}} min), where the discrete quantile index makes the conformal windows conservative (Table S6).

## Efficiency: interval score and width

Among the methods with valid coverage, the proposed windows were the narrowest (median width {{main.P.width_median_min|.2f}} min, i.e. {{main.P.width_rel_median|pct0}} of the system's RT span, versus {{main.B5_jackknife_plus.width_median_min|.2f}} min for jackknife+, {{main.B4_split_conformal.width_median_min|.2f}} min for split conformal prediction and {{main.B6_jk+_ensemble.width_median_min|.2f}} min for jackknife+ normalised by ensemble spread), and they achieved the lowest mean interval score of all methods ({{main.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} min; Table 2, Fig. 2c). Paired by system, the interval score of the proposed windows was lower than that of jackknife+ by a median of {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.median_diff|.2f}} min (95 % CI {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.diff_ci_lo|.2f}} to {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.diff_ci_hi|.2f}}; rank-biserial correlation {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.rank_biserial|.2f}}; Holm-adjusted $p$ {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.p_holm|p3}}), and lower than those of the Gaussian, P-spline and GP windows in {{tests.winkler.B1_gauss_rmse.share_ref_better|pct0}}, {{tests.winkler.B2_pspline_PI.share_ref_better|pct0}} and {{tests.winkler.B3_gp_PI.share_ref_better|pct0}} of the systems, respectively (Table 3; all calibrant numbers and α levels in Table S4). The parametric windows were narrower still (median {{main.B3_gp_PI.width_median_min|.2f}}–{{main.B1_gauss_rmse.width_median_min|.2f}} min), but this narrowness is precisely what made them under-cover, and the interval score penalises the resulting misses. Normalising jackknife+ by the spread of bootstrap-replicate base models — a common heuristic uncertainty signal — over-covered ({{main.B6_jk+_ensemble.coverage_mean|.3f}}) and nearly doubled the median window width ({{main.B6_jk+_ensemble.width_median_min|.2f}} versus {{main.B5_jackknife_plus.width_median_min|.2f}} min; mean width {{main.B6_jk+_ensemble.width_mean_min|.1f}} versus {{main.B5_jackknife_plus.width_mean_min|.1f}} min), showing that the choice of the scale function matters.

## Heterogeneity of the gain across systems

The improvement over plain jackknife+ was not uniform (Table 3). The interval score decreased by a median of {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Folberth.median_rel_change|pct}} in the Folberth systems (better in {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Folberth.share_P_better|pct0}} of them) and by {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Souihi+Stoffel.median_rel_change|pct}} in the Souihi and Stoffel systems, whereas the median change was {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.BGC.median_rel_change|pct}} in the BGC systems ($p$ = {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.BGC.p_wilcoxon|.2f}}), {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.PredRet.median_rel_change|pct}} in PredRet and {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Other sources.median_rel_change|pct}} in the remaining sources. Against the Gaussian window, the proposed windows were even slightly worse in the BGC fold ({{het.B1_gauss_rmse.BGC.median_rel_change|pct}}; $p$ = {{het.B1_gauss_rmse.BGC.p_wilcoxon|.3f}}), although the Gaussian window under-covered there as elsewhere. The relative gain over jackknife+ increased with the RT span of the system (Spearman $\rho$ = {{span.spearman_rho|.2f}}, $p$ {{span.p|p3}}), partly because the RT span differs between source groups (within folds, $\rho$ = {{span.within_fold_rho_min|.2f}} to {{span.within_fold_rho_max|.2f}}; fold-adjusted partial $\rho$ = {{span.partial_rho_fold_adjusted|.2f}}, $p$ = {{span.partial_p|.3f}}): systems whose RT span exceeded the median of {{span.median_span_min|.1f}} min gained {{span.median_rel_change_span_above_median|pct}}, whereas shorter systems changed by {{span.median_rel_change_span_below_median|pct}}. A plausible but untested explanation, consistent with the conditional-coverage results below, is that long gradients produce more heteroscedastic transfer errors (late eluters and compounds outside the calibrant range are mapped less precisely), which the difficulty model can capture, whereas in short gradients the errors may be closer to homoscedastic, so that estimating a scale function mainly adds variance. Practically, plain jackknife+ is an adequate and simpler choice for short gradients, and the normalised version pays off as runs get longer.

## Number of calibrants

Conformal windows are finite only if $n\ge 1/\alpha-1$. Accordingly, all jackknife+-type windows were infinite at $\alpha=0.05$ for $n=10$ and $15$, and split conformal prediction, which calibrates on only half of the calibrants, was also infinite at $\alpha=0.10$ for $n\le 15$ (Fig. 3, Table S2). Above these limits, the mean coverage of the proposed windows at $\alpha=0.10$ was {{s1.P.n40.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}}–{{s1.P.n15.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}}; the small excess at $n=15$ ({{s1.P.n15.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}}) is the expected conservativeness of the discrete quantile index, whose effective level is $k_{\mathrm{u}}/(n+1)=15/16$. The median width of the proposed windows decreased from {{s1.P.n10.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=10$ to {{s1.P.n20.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=20$ and {{s1.P.n40.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=40$, and it was smaller than that of jackknife+ at every $n$ ({{s1.B5_jackknife_plus.n10.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}}, {{s1.B5_jackknife_plus.n20.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} and {{s1.B5_jackknife_plus.n40.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min). For 95 % windows, the proposed method gave a median width of {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=20$ and {{s1.P.n40.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=40$ with coverage {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{s1.P.n40.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}. With fewer calibrants than the bound (exploratory runs with $n=5$ and $8$; Table S5), conformal windows at $\alpha=0.10$ were infinite, i.e. the data honestly did not support the requested error rate, whereas the parametric intervals remained finite but became severely over-confident: GP and P-spline intervals covered {{sn.B3_gp_PI.n5.a0.10.coverage_mean|pct0}} and {{sn.B2_pspline_PI.n5.a0.10.coverage_mean|pct0}} of the true RTs at $n=5$, and the Gaussian window {{sn.B1_gauss_rmse.n5.a0.10.coverage_mean|pct0}}. At $\alpha=0.20$, for which eight calibrants suffice, the proposed windows were valid ({{sn.P.n8.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}} at $n=8$) while GP intervals covered {{sn.B3_gp_PI.n8.a0.20.coverage_mean|pct0}}. Because about 20 calibrants are the minimum for a finite 95 % window and widths at $\alpha$ = 0.10 kept shrinking up to the largest calibrant set tested (40; for 95 % windows {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}}, {{s1.P.n30.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}} and {{s1.P.n40.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n$ = 20, 30 and 40), laboratories that rely on RT filtering benefit from calibrant mixes of at least 20, and preferably about 40, compounds.

## Conditional coverage

The guarantee concerns the average over the compounds of a system; it does not ensure coverage within every compound group (Fig. 4, Tables S9–S11). The groups that matter most for NTS were also the most difficult. For compounds whose base score lay outside the range spanned by the calibrants, coverage fell to {{strata.region.outside_cal_range.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}} with jackknife+ and {{strata.region.outside_cal_range.B1_gauss_rmse|.3f}} with the Gaussian window, but was {{strata.region.outside_cal_range.P|.3f}} with the proposed windows; for compounds eluting in the last third of the system's RT range ({{strata_share.rt_tertile.late|pct0}} of test compounds) the values were {{strata.rt_tertile.late.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}}, {{strata.rt_tertile.late.B1_gauss_rmse|.3f}} and {{strata.rt_tertile.late.P|.3f}}, and for compounds absent from the base model's training data {{strata.seen.unseen.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}}, {{strata.seen.unseen.B1_gauss_rmse|.3f}} and {{strata.seen.unseen.P|.3f}}. Inside the calibrant range the proposed windows were slightly less conservative than jackknife+ ({{strata.region.inside_cal_range.P|.3f}} vs {{strata.region.inside_cal_range.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}}): the difficulty model moves width from easy to difficult compounds, which is the intended behaviour. Coverage nevertheless remained below nominal for compounds that were neither seen by the base model nor similar to its training data (Tanimoto 0.4–0.7: {{strata.similarity.sim0.4-0.7.P|.3f}}; < 0.4: {{strata.similarity.sim<0.4.P|.3f}}), so windows for structurally novel candidates should be interpreted with caution or widened, for example by calibrating them on a Mondrian partition by novelty [@vovk2013conditional] when enough calibrants are available.

## Candidate filtering with isomeric decoys

On average, {{dec.parents_per_system|int}} test compounds per system and draw had at least one isomeric decoy, with {{dec.decoys_per_system|int}} decoys per system and draw (Table S13). The proposed 90 % windows retained the correct candidate for {{dec.P.0.10.ret|pct}} of the features and eliminated {{dec.P.0.10.elim|pct}} of the decoys, not significantly different from jackknife+ ({{dec.B5_jackknife_plus.0.10.ret|pct}} and {{dec.B5_jackknife_plus.0.10.elim|pct}}; $p$ = {{dectest.B5_jackknife_plus.0.10.p|.2f}} for decoy elimination; Table S14). At the same nominal level, the parametric windows eliminated more decoys (Gaussian {{dec.B1_gauss_rmse.0.10.elim|pct}}, P-spline {{dec.B2_pspline_PI.0.10.elim|pct}}, GP {{dec.B3_gp_PI.0.10.elim|pct}}) mainly because they also discarded more correct structures (retention {{dec.B1_gauss_rmse.0.10.ret|pct}}, {{dec.B2_pspline_PI.0.10.ret|pct}} and {{dec.B3_gp_PI.0.10.ret|pct}}). When compared at matched retention, all constructions lay on nearly the same trade-off curve (Fig. 5), with the parametric windows slightly ahead: at 85 % retention, decoy elimination ranged from {{mr.P.0.85|pct}} (proposed) and {{mr.B5_jackknife_plus.0.85|pct}} (jackknife+) to {{mr.B1_gauss_rmse.0.85|pct}} (Gaussian), {{mr.B3_gp_PI.0.85|pct}} (GP) and {{mr.B2_pspline_PI.0.85|pct}} (P-spline), and at 90 % retention from {{mr.P.0.90|pct}} (proposed) to {{mr.B1_gauss_rmse.0.90|pct}} (Gaussian), a level the GP and P-spline intervals did not reach at any tested $\alpha$ (Table S15). Fixed ±2 min windows retained a similar share of correct structures ({{fwdec.FIX_2min.ret|pct}}) but eliminated fewer decoys ({{fwdec.FIX_2min.elim|pct}}) than the model-based windows at 85 % retention (Table S16). Conformal windows therefore do not filter more efficiently; their contribution is that the operating point is known before the analysis: a laboratory that chooses $\alpha=0.10$ obtains about 90 % retention of correct structures on its own system, whereas the retention delivered by a nominal 90 % parametric interval is unknown in advance and was, on average, {{dec.B3_gp_PI.0.10.ret|pct}} for GP projection. RT filtering alone removed only about a quarter to a third of isomeric decoys at these retention levels, which reiterates that RT is a supporting rather than a decisive line of evidence for isomeric candidates [@akhlaqi2023complementary] and is best combined with MS/MS and CCS evidence [@metz2025identification; @ferrandoplo2026candidate].

## Ablation of the difficulty model

Every feature group contributed (Fig. 6, Table S17). Removing the physicochemical, calibrant-geometry, system, novelty or ionisation features increased the per-system interval score by a median (paired by system) of {{abl.P-compound.median_diff|.2f}}, {{abl.P-geometry.median_diff|.2f}}, {{abl.P-system.median_diff|.2f}}, {{abl.P-novelty.median_diff|.2f}} and {{abl.P-ion.median_diff|.2f}} min, respectively (all Holm-adjusted $p$ < 0.001), while marginal coverage was unchanged, as guaranteed. Two design choices were essential. Computing the scores from in-sample residuals of the full calibrant fit instead of leave-one-out refits — a tempting shortcut — reduced mean coverage to {{abl.P_naive_noLOO.coverage_mean|.3f}}, an empirical confirmation that the leave-one-out construction is what makes the guarantee hold. Replacing the monotone map by a linear one increased the interval score by {{abl.P_linear_map.median_diff|.2f}} min, reflecting the curvature of RT mappings between gradients. The results were insensitive to the difficulty-model hyper-parameters: with 15 or 63 leaves (400 trees) or 200 or 800 trees (31 leaves), the mean interval score ranged from {{sens.sens_l63_e400.P.winkler|.2f}} to {{sens.sens_l15_e400.P.winkler|.2f}} min at unchanged coverage, always below that of jackknife+ (Table S18).

## Robustness to calibrant selection and calibrant errors

Calibrant selection is where the exchangeability assumption is most likely to fail in practice (Table 4, Fig. 7, Table S19). When the calibrants were chosen to span the RT range, as is usual when a calibrant mix is designed, the conformal windows became slightly conservative ({{rob.span.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the proposed windows and {{rob.span.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+): spreading the calibrants left fewer test compounds outside their range ({{mech.span.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} versus {{mech.main.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} for random calibrants; Table S20), whereas the leave-one-out residuals of the extreme calibrants are themselves extrapolations. Noisy calibrant RTs (Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 2 % of the RT span) and a single outlying calibrant (shifted by 10 % of the span) also made the conformal windows conservative ({{rob.noise.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{rob.outlier.P.coverage_mean|.3f}}), because calibrant errors inflate the calibration residuals; GP intervals remained invalid in all three scenarios ({{rob.outlier.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}–{{rob.span.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}).

The decisive failure occurred when the calibrants were the 20 compounds of each system that are most frequently measured across RepoRT, a proxy for mixes of well-known reference standards. All windows except the very wide jackknife+ normalised by ensemble spread ({{rob.common.B6_jk+_ensemble.coverage_mean|.3f}}; median width {{rob.common.B6_jk+_ensemble.width_median_min|.1f}} min) then under-covered markedly: {{rob.common.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the proposed windows, {{rob.common.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+, {{rob.common.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian window and {{rob.common.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for GP intervals. Two mechanisms were at work (Table S20). Commonly measured compounds cluster in part of the elution range, so that {{mech.common.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} of the test compounds lay outside the calibrant score range (versus {{mech.main.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} with random calibrants); and they are presumably easier to predict than typical analytes (for example, more often present in the base model's training data), so that coverage fell even inside the calibrant range ({{mech.common.P.cov_inside|.3f}}). The difficulty model mitigated the first mechanism — coverage outside the calibrant range was {{mech.common.P.cov_outside|.3f}} with the proposed windows versus {{mech.common.B5_jackknife_plus.cov_outside|.3f}} with jackknife+ — but could not remove the bias. Calibrant mixes therefore need to span the RT range and to resemble the analytes; when a fixed selection rule is used, the correction described in the next sections is required.

## Generalisation to another base model and to HILIC

Replacing the multi-system retention-order model by a gradient-boosted regressor trained on the METLIN SMRT system only — the common "pre-train on SMRT, then project" approach — left the validity conclusions unchanged (at $n=20$: proposed {{gen.smrt.n20.P.coverage_mean|.3f}}, jackknife+ {{gen.smrt.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}}, Gaussian {{gen.smrt.n20.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}}, GP {{gen.smrt.n20.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}; Table 4, Table S21) and the proposed windows kept their advantage (mean interval score {{gen.smrt.n20.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} versus {{gen.smrt.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.winkler_mean|.2f}} min for jackknife+; median paired difference {{gentest.gen_smrt.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.median_diff|.2f}} min, better in {{gentest.gen_smrt.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.share_ref_better|pct0}} of the systems). The windows were wider than with the retention-order model (median {{gen.smrt.n20.P.width_median_min|.2f}} versus {{main.P.width_median_min|.2f}} min), consistent with the better transferability of retention order [@kretschmer2026order], although the two base models also differ in their training data (SMRT only versus RepoRT systems of the other sources plus SMRT). On {{gen.hilic.n20.P.n_systems|int}} HILIC systems, with base and difficulty models trained on HILIC data from other sources only, the conformal windows again had nominal coverage ({{gen.hilic.n20.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} proposed, {{gen.hilic.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} jackknife+) and the GP intervals again under-covered ({{gen.hilic.n20.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}). In HILIC, however, the difficulty model increased the interval score ({{gen.hilic.n20.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} versus {{gen.hilic.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.winkler_mean|.2f}} min for jackknife+): with {{tsz.HILIC.ge30.min}}–{{tsz.HILIC.ge30.max}} heterogeneous training systems per fold, compared with {{tsz.RP.ge30.min}}–{{tsz.RP.ge30.max}} in reversed phase, the meta-learned scale function is possibly too noisy to be useful, although HILIC also differs in its retention mechanism. Together with the short-gradient results, this delimits the method: the validity of the conformal construction was maintained with both base models and both separation modes tested, whereas the efficiency gain of the learned normalisation appears to require a large and representative collection of training systems; where such a collection is lacking, plain jackknife+ should be used.

## External validation on interlaboratory NTS data

The NORMAN interlaboratory comparison provides retention times of a common set of calibration chemicals and spiked suspects measured with {{norman.n_systems|int}} routine NTS methods in different laboratories [@malm2025projection; @malm2024norman] and thus a test of the whole workflow on retention data that played no role in model development (models trained on all RepoRT reversed-phase systems, which contain {{norman.seen_share|pct0}} of the NORMAN compounds; Addendum C). When calibrants were drawn at random from all compounds, the result of the main study was reproduced (Table 4; Table S22): with 20 calibrants, the conformal windows covered {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} (proposed) and {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} (jackknife+) of the true RTs, against {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}}, {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.B2_pspline_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian, P-spline and GP windows. On these external systems, however, the difficulty model brought no efficiency gain (mean interval score {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} versus {{nor.random_all.20.a0.10.B5_jackknife_plus.winkler_mean|.2f}} min for jackknife+; $p$ = {{nortest.random_all.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.p_wilcoxon|.2f}}), and with calibration chemicals as calibrants it reduced efficiency (Holm-adjusted $p$ = {{nortest.fixed_mix.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.p_holm|.2f}} and {{nortest.pool_random.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.p_holm|.2f}}; Table S22). Missing descriptors are unlikely to be the main reason: column temperature, flow rate and dead time, which the NORMAN methods do not report, were also missing for {{mdmiss.temp|int}}, {{mdmiss.flow|int}} and {{mdmiss.t0|int}} of the {{mdmiss.n_systems|int}} reversed-phase RepoRT systems (all three for {{mdmiss.all3|int}} {{mdmiss.all3_sources}} systems), and missing values enter the difficulty model identically in both data sets (Section S1).

The realistic use case — calibrating on the laboratory's calibration chemicals and screening the suspects — revealed the limits of the exchangeability assumption. With the full set of detected calibration chemicals ({{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.P.n_systems|int}} systems, {{nor.mix_n.min|int}}–{{nor.mix_n.max|int}} calibrants each), mean coverage of the suspects' RTs was {{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} (95 % CI {{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.P.coverage_ci_lo|.3f}}–{{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.P.coverage_ci_hi|.3f}}) for the proposed windows and {{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+, i.e. below the nominal 0.90 but still above the GP intervals ({{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}); split conformal windows, which are wider, reached {{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.B4_split_conformal.coverage_mean|.3f}}. Random subsets of 20 calibration chemicals ({{nor.pool_random.20.a0.10.P.n_systems|int}} systems with enough detected calibration chemicals) gave the same picture ({{nor.pool_random.20.a0.10.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{nor.pool_random.20.a0.10.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}}). The calibration chemicals and the suspects are different compound populations, and a procedure that uses only the calibrants' retention data cannot detect how much harder the latter are to predict; covariate-shift-aware conformal methods that weight the calibrants by their similarity to the candidates [@tibshirani2019weighted; @barber2023beyond] are a possible remedy.

## Selection-aware system-level calibration

When the rule by which calibrants are chosen is known, it can be replayed on other chromatographic systems, which are then the exchangeable units (Addendum D). For every leave-source-out fold, the factor $c$ by which the scaled scores $\sigma_{-i}(x)R_i$ must be multiplied so that the *average* coverage over {{sascc.common.ntrain.min}}–{{sascc.common.ntrain.max}} training systems reaches $1-\alpha$ was {{sascc.common.P.min|.2f}}–{{sascc.common.P.max|.2f}} for the commonly-measured-calibrant rule (jackknife+: {{sascc.common.J.min|.2f}}–{{sascc.common.J.max|.2f}}), {{sascc.span.P.min|.2f}}–{{sascc.span.P.max|.2f}} for RT-spanning calibrants and {{sascc.random.P.min|.2f}}–{{sascc.random.P.max|.2f}} for random calibrants (Table S25). Applied to the target systems (Table 4; Table S24), this selection-aware calibration restored the coverage under the commonly-measured-calibrant rule from {{sasc.common.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} to {{sasc.common.P+SASC.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the proposed windows (jackknife+: from {{sasc.common.J+.coverage_mean|.3f}} to {{sasc.common.J+SASC.coverage_mean|.3f}}), at the price of wider windows (median width {{sasc.common.P+SASC.width_median_min|.2f}} versus {{sasc.common.P.width_median_min|.2f}} min); for RT-spanning calibrants it removed the conservativeness and narrowed the windows from {{sasc.span.P.width_median_min|.2f}} to {{sasc.span.P+SASC.width_median_min|.2f}} min at a coverage of {{sasc.span.P+SASC.coverage_mean|.3f}}. Because the factor equalises the average over systems rather than the coverage of each system [@bian2023training], individual systems still varied ({{sasc.common.P+SASC.share_severe_under|pct0}} of the systems remained below 0.85 under the common-calibrant rule). The correction failed where the analyte population, rather than the calibrant selection, differed: on the {{sascN.n_systems|int}} RepoRT systems containing at least ten NORMAN calibration chemicals, those chemicals were representative calibrants ($c$ = {{sascN.c_P|.2f}} for the proposed windows and {{sascN.c_J|.2f}} for jackknife+), and applying these factors to the NORMAN suspects lowered coverage further ({{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.P+SASC.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{nor.fixed_mix.mix.a0.10.J+SASC.coverage_mean|.3f}}). Selection-aware calibration therefore corrects a known selection rule, not a shift in the kind of compounds being screened; the latter requires calibrants that resemble the analytes.

## Error analysis

With the proposed 90 % windows ($n=20$, first draw), {{err.miss_rate|pct}} of test compounds fell outside their window, and {{err.late_share|pct0}} of these misses eluted later than the window. Among the 15 classes with the most misses, miss rates were highest for phenols ({{errcls.Phenols|pct}}), quinolines ({{errcls.Quinolines and derivatives|pct}}), organonitrogen compounds ({{errcls.Organonitrogen compounds|pct}}), flavonoids ({{errcls.Flavonoids|pct}}) and pyridines ({{errcls.Pyridines and derivatives|pct}}) and lowest for indoles ({{errcls.Indoles and derivatives|pct}}), fatty acyls ({{errcls.Fatty Acyls|pct}}) and steroids ({{errcls.Steroids and steroid derivatives|pct}}) (Table S12). Several of these classes contain basic nitrogen whose protonation, and hence retention, can depend on mobile-phase pH and column chemistry — selectivity changes that a monotone transfer of a system-independent score cannot represent [@malm2025projection; @kretschmer2026order]; this interpretation was not tested directly, and coverage of the proposed windows did not differ between ionisable and neutral compounds ({{strata.ionisable.ionisable.P|.3f}} versus {{strata.ionisable.neutral.P|.3f}}).

## Computational cost

On a single CPU thread, training the retention-order base model on {{cb.base_rows|int}} retention times took {{cb.base_train_min|.1f}} min and training the difficulty model on {{cb.sigma_rows|int}} simulated residuals {{cb.sigma_train_s|.1f}} s (the fold-specific models of this study used {{cb.sigma_rows_fold_min|int}}–{{cb.sigma_rows_fold_max|int}} residuals); both are done once, on public data. Calibrating a new system — the leave-one-out maps and difficulty scales of the calibrants — took {{cb.n10.cal_ms|.0f}}, {{cb.n20.cal_ms|.0f}} and {{cb.n40.cal_ms|.0f}} ms for 10, 20 and 40 calibrants, and computing the window of a candidate structure {{cb.n20.us_per_cand|.0f}} µs at $n=20$ once its descriptors and base score are available, so that the conformal step for 10,000 candidates takes a few seconds (featurisation and base-model scoring excluded; peak resident memory of the benchmark process, including base-model training, {{cb.peak_rss_MB|.0f}} MB). The conformal layer thus adds no practical cost to an NTS workflow; its only real cost is the requirement of enough, representative calibrants.

## Practical recommendations

(i) Do not use fixed, proportional or parametric RT tolerances when a model is transferred to a new system: their retention of correct structures is unknown and often far below the nominal value. (ii) Measure at least $1/\alpha-1$ calibrants — 19 for a 95 % window — and preferably about 40, spread over the elution range and chosen to resemble the analytes. (iii) Use calibrant-based jackknife+ windows; the meta-learned normalisation is worthwhile for long reversed-phase gradients, whereas plain jackknife+ suffices for short gradients and HILIC. (iv) If the calibrants follow a fixed rule (e.g. a standard mix of common compounds), calibrate the windows by replaying that rule on public data, and treat structurally novel candidates and analyte classes absent from the calibrants with caution. (v) Combine the RT window with MS/MS and CCS evidence rather than using it as the sole filter.

## Limitations

The finite-sample guarantee is marginal and rests on exchangeability of calibrants and analytes; it does not extend to fixed calibrant mixes that differ from the analytes, as the NORMAN results show, and it does not imply coverage within compound classes (structurally novel compounds remained under-covered). The selection-aware correction gives an average-over-systems, not a per-system, guarantee and requires that the selection rule and the analyte population can be replayed on public data. The efficiency gain of the learned normalisation was context-dependent (long reversed-phase gradients) and absent in short gradients, HILIC and the external systems. RepoRT is dominated by metabolite standards and by a few large sources whose systems share compounds and laboratories; we used leave-source-out validation, but systems within a source are not independent, and a cluster bootstrap over five sources is necessarily coarse. Isomeric decoys were RepoRT compounds rather than candidate lists from structure databases, and no prospective, wet-laboratory NTS study was performed. Finally, the base model does not encode chromatographic conditions; stronger, condition-aware base models [@kretschmer2026order] could narrow the windows, and the conformal layer applies to them unchanged.
