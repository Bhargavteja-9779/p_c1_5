# Results and discussion

## Coverage of current retention-time windows on new systems

With $n=20$ randomly drawn calibrants and a nominal 90 % window, the parametric windows retained the correct structure less often than intended (Table 2, Fig. 2a). Mean coverage over the {{n_target_systems}} target systems was {{main.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for Gaussian-process projection intervals, {{main.B2_pspline_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for P-spline prediction intervals and {{main.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian ±z·RMSE window, and coverage fell below 0.85 in {{main.B3_gp_PI.share_severe_under|pct0}}, {{main.B2_pspline_PI.share_severe_under|pct0}} and {{main.B1_gauss_rmse.share_severe_under|pct0}} of the systems, respectively. The deficit grew as calibrants became scarcer: at $n=10$, mean coverage was {{s1.B3_gp_PI.n10.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}} (GP), {{s1.B2_pspline_PI.n10.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}} (P-spline) and {{s1.B1_gauss_rmse.n10.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}} (Gaussian) (Fig. 3a). In contrast, every conformal construction reached the nominal level on average: {{main.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+, {{main.B4_split_conformal.coverage_mean|.3f}} for split conformal prediction and {{main.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} (95 % CI {{main.P.coverage_ci_lo|.3f}}–{{main.P.coverage_ci_hi|.3f}}) for the proposed windows, and no target system had a mean coverage below 0.85 with the proposed windows. The conformal windows tracked the nominal level at every $\alpha$ (at $n=20$: {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}} for $\alpha=0.05$ and {{s1.P.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}} for $\alpha=0.20$), whereas the parametric windows were miscalibrated in an $\alpha$-dependent direction (Fig. S2, Table S1): at $\alpha=0.05$ all three under-covered (Gaussian {{s1.B1_gauss_rmse.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}, P-spline {{s1.B2_pspline_PI.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}, GP {{s1.B3_gp_PI.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}), while at $\alpha=0.20$ the Gaussian window over-covered ({{s1.B1_gauss_rmse.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}}) and the GP and P-spline intervals still under-covered ({{s1.B3_gp_PI.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{s1.B2_pspline_PI.n20.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}}). Such level-dependent errors are the signature of heavy-tailed transfer residuals, for which a normal quantile is too wide near the centre and too narrow in the tails. Coverage of the conformal windows was also at the nominal level in each of the five source folds (Table S4), and a cluster bootstrap over source folds gave practically identical confidence intervals (Table S2). These results confirm H1 and H2: windows derived from parametric error models transfer their optimism to new systems, whereas calibrant-based conformal windows keep the promised error rate without any target-system training data.

Fixed tolerances, which are still widely used, fared worse (Table 9; evaluated on the same calibrant draws, post hoc). With 20 calibrants, windows of ±0.25, ±0.5, ±1 and ±2 min retained the correct structure in {{fw.FIX_0.25min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}}, {{fw.FIX_0.5min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}}, {{fw.FIX_1min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}} and {{fw.FIX_2min.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}} of cases on average, and relative windows of ±5 % and ±10 % of the predicted RT in {{fw.FIX_5pct.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}} and {{fw.FIX_10pct.n20.coverage_mean|pct0}}. A fixed window cannot adapt to systems whose RT errors differ by an order of magnitude: even ±2 min kept fewer than 85 % of the correct structures in {{fw.FIX_2min.n20.share_severe_under|pct0}} of the systems. Residual-based windows of ±2·RMSE and ±3·RMSE, often read as approximately 95 % and 99.7 % intervals, delivered {{fw.RMSE_x2.n20.coverage_mean|pct}} and {{fw.RMSE_x3.n20.coverage_mean|pct}} at $n=20$ and {{fw.RMSE_x2.n10.coverage_mean|pct}} for ±2·RMSE at $n=10$. In terms of the interval score, the proposed windows were better than every fixed or residual-based tolerance at every calibrant number (Table 9).

## Efficiency: interval score and width

Among the methods with valid coverage, the proposed windows were the narrowest (median width {{main.P.width_median_min|.2f}} min, i.e. {{main.P.width_rel_median|pct0}} of the system's RT span, versus {{main.B5_jackknife_plus.width_median_min|.2f}} min for jackknife+, {{main.B4_split_conformal.width_median_min|.2f}} min for split conformal prediction and {{main.B6_jk+_ensemble.width_median_min|.2f}} min for jackknife+ normalised by ensemble spread), and they achieved the lowest mean interval score of all methods ({{main.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} min; Table 2, Fig. 2c). Paired by system, the interval score of the proposed windows was lower than that of jackknife+ by a median of {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.median_diff|.2f}} min (95 % CI {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.diff_ci_lo|.2f}} to {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.diff_ci_hi|.2f}}; rank-biserial correlation {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.rank_biserial|.2f}}; Holm-adjusted $p$ {{tests.winkler.B5_jackknife_plus.p_holm|p3}}), and lower than those of the Gaussian, P-spline and GP windows in {{tests.winkler.B1_gauss_rmse.share_ref_better|pct0}}, {{tests.winkler.B2_pspline_PI.share_ref_better|pct0}} and {{tests.winkler.B3_gp_PI.share_ref_better|pct0}} of the systems, respectively (Table 3). The parametric windows were narrower still (median {{main.B3_gp_PI.width_median_min|.2f}}–{{main.B1_gauss_rmse.width_median_min|.2f}} min), but this narrowness is precisely what made them under-cover, and the interval score penalises the resulting misses. Normalising jackknife+ by the spread of bootstrap-replicate base models — a common heuristic uncertainty signal — over-covered and more than doubled the window width, showing that the choice of the scale function matters.

## Heterogeneity of the gain across systems

The improvement over plain jackknife+ was not uniform (Table 11). The interval score decreased by a median of {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Folberth.median_rel_change|pct}} in the Folberth systems (better in {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Folberth.share_P_better|pct0}} of them) and by {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Souihi+Stoffel.median_rel_change|pct}} in the Souihi and Stoffel systems, whereas the median change was {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.BGC.median_rel_change|pct}} in the BGC systems ($p$ = {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.BGC.p_wilcoxon|.2f}}), {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.PredRet.median_rel_change|pct}} in PredRet and {{het.B5_jackknife_plus.Other sources.median_rel_change|pct}} in the remaining sources. Against the Gaussian window, the proposed windows were even slightly worse in the BGC fold ({{het.B1_gauss_rmse.BGC.median_rel_change|pct}}; $p$ = {{het.B1_gauss_rmse.BGC.p_wilcoxon|.3f}}), although the Gaussian window under-covered there as elsewhere. The relative gain over jackknife+ increased with the length of the chromatographic run (Spearman $\rho$ = {{span.spearman_rho|.2f}}, $p$ {{span.p|p3}}): systems whose RT span exceeded the median of {{span.median_span_min|.1f}} min gained {{span.median_rel_change_span_above_median|pct}}, whereas shorter systems changed by {{span.median_rel_change_span_below_median|pct}}. A plausible explanation, consistent with the conditional-coverage results below, is that long gradients produce markedly heteroscedastic transfer errors (late eluters and compounds outside the calibrant range are mapped less precisely), which the difficulty model captures, whereas in short gradients the errors are nearly homoscedastic and estimating a scale function adds variance without reducing bias. Practically, plain jackknife+ is an adequate and simpler choice for short gradients, and the normalised version pays off as runs get longer.

## Number of calibrants

Conformal windows are finite only if $n\ge 1/\alpha-1$. Accordingly, all jackknife+-type windows were infinite at $\alpha=0.05$ for $n=10$ and $15$, and split conformal prediction, which calibrates on only half of the calibrants, was also infinite at $\alpha=0.10$ for $n\le 15$ (Fig. 3, Table S1). Above these limits, the mean coverage of the proposed windows at $\alpha=0.10$ was {{s1.P.n20.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}}–{{s1.P.n15.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}}; the small excess at $n=15$ ({{s1.P.n15.a0.10.coverage_mean|.3f}}) is the expected conservativeness of the discrete quantile index, whose effective level is $\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil/(n+1)=15/16$. The median width of the proposed windows decreased from {{s1.P.n10.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=10$ to {{s1.P.n20.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=20$ and {{s1.P.n40.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=40$, and it was smaller than that of jackknife+ at every $n$ ({{s1.B5_jackknife_plus.n10.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}}, {{s1.B5_jackknife_plus.n20.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} and {{s1.B5_jackknife_plus.n40.a0.10.width_median_min|.2f}} min). For 95 % windows, the proposed method gave a median width of {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=20$ and {{s1.P.n40.a0.05.width_median_min|.2f}} min at $n=40$ with coverage {{s1.P.n20.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{s1.P.n40.a0.05.coverage_mean|.3f}}. With fewer calibrants than the bound (exploratory runs with $n=5$ and $8$; Table S12), conformal windows at $\alpha=0.10$ were infinite, i.e. the data honestly did not support the requested error rate, whereas the parametric intervals remained finite but became severely over-confident: GP and P-spline intervals covered {{sn.B3_gp_PI.n5.a0.10.coverage_mean|pct0}} and {{sn.B2_pspline_PI.n5.a0.10.coverage_mean|pct0}} of the true RTs at $n=5$, and the Gaussian window {{sn.B1_gauss_rmse.n5.a0.10.coverage_mean|pct0}}. At $\alpha=0.20$, for which eight calibrants suffice, the proposed windows were valid ({{sn.P.n8.a0.20.coverage_mean|.3f}} at $n=8$) while GP intervals covered {{sn.B3_gp_PI.n8.a0.20.coverage_mean|pct0}}. Because about 20 calibrants are the minimum for a finite 95 % window and widths kept shrinking up to the largest calibrant set tested (40), laboratories that rely on RT filtering benefit from calibrant mixes of at least 20, and preferably 30–40, compounds.

## Conditional coverage

The guarantee concerns the average over the compounds of a system; it does not ensure coverage within every compound group (Fig. 4, Table S5). The groups that matter most for NTS were also the most difficult. For compounds whose base score lay outside the range spanned by the calibrants, coverage fell to {{strata.region.outside_cal_range.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}} with jackknife+ and {{strata.region.outside_cal_range.B1_gauss_rmse|.3f}} with the Gaussian window, but was {{strata.region.outside_cal_range.P|.3f}} with the proposed windows; for the late-eluting third of compounds the values were {{strata.rt_tertile.late.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}}, {{strata.rt_tertile.late.B1_gauss_rmse|.3f}} and {{strata.rt_tertile.late.P|.3f}}, and for compounds absent from the base model's training data {{strata.seen.unseen.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}}, {{strata.seen.unseen.B1_gauss_rmse|.3f}} and {{strata.seen.unseen.P|.3f}}. Inside the calibrant range the proposed windows were slightly less conservative than jackknife+ ({{strata.region.inside_cal_range.P|.3f}} vs {{strata.region.inside_cal_range.B5_jackknife_plus|.3f}}): the difficulty model moves width from easy to difficult compounds, which is the intended behaviour. Coverage nevertheless remained below nominal for compounds that were neither seen by the base model nor similar to its training data (Tanimoto 0.4–0.7: {{strata.similarity.sim0.4-0.7.P|.3f}}; < 0.4: {{strata.similarity.sim<0.4.P|.3f}}), so windows for structurally novel candidates should be interpreted with caution or widened, for example by calibrating them on a Mondrian partition by novelty when enough calibrants are available.

## Candidate filtering with isomeric decoys

Each test compound had on average {{dec.decoys_per_system|int}} isomeric decoys per system in the evaluation (Table 7). The proposed 90 % windows retained the correct candidate for {{dec.P.0.10.ret|pct}} of the features and eliminated {{dec.P.0.10.elim|pct}} of the decoys, indistinguishable from jackknife+ ({{dec.B5_jackknife_plus.0.10.ret|pct}} and {{dec.B5_jackknife_plus.0.10.elim|pct}}; $p$ = {{dectest.B5_jackknife_plus.0.10.p|.2f}}). At the same nominal level, the parametric windows eliminated more decoys (Gaussian {{dec.B1_gauss_rmse.0.10.elim|pct}}, P-spline {{dec.B2_pspline_PI.0.10.elim|pct}}, GP {{dec.B3_gp_PI.0.10.elim|pct}}) only because they also discarded more correct structures (retention {{dec.B1_gauss_rmse.0.10.ret|pct}}, {{dec.B2_pspline_PI.0.10.ret|pct}} and {{dec.B3_gp_PI.0.10.ret|pct}}). When compared at matched retention, all constructions lay on nearly the same trade-off curve (Fig. 5): at 85 % retention, decoy elimination ranged from {{mr.P.0.85|pct}} (proposed) to {{mr.B2_pspline_PI.0.85|pct}} (P-spline), and at 90 % retention from {{mr.P.0.90|pct}} (proposed) to {{mr.B1_gauss_rmse.0.90|pct}} (Gaussian), a level the GP and P-spline intervals did not reach at any tested $\alpha$ (Table S14). Conformal windows therefore do not filter more efficiently; their contribution is that the operating point is known before the analysis: a laboratory that chooses $\alpha=0.10$ obtains about 90 % retention of correct structures on its own system, whereas the retention delivered by a nominal 90 % parametric interval is unknown in advance and was, on average, {{dec.B3_gp_PI.0.10.ret|pct}} for GP projection. RT filtering alone removed only about a quarter to a third of isomeric decoys at these retention levels, which reiterates that RT is a supporting rather than a decisive line of evidence and is best combined with MS/MS and CCS evidence [@metz2025identification; @ferrandoplo2026candidate].

## Ablation of the difficulty model

Every feature group contributed (Table 4, Fig. 6). Removing the physicochemical, calibrant-geometry, system, novelty or ionisation features increased the median interval score by {{abl.P-compound.median_diff|.2f}}, {{abl.P-geometry.median_diff|.2f}}, {{abl.P-system.median_diff|.2f}}, {{abl.P-novelty.median_diff|.2f}} and {{abl.P-ion.median_diff|.2f}} min, respectively (all Holm-adjusted $p$ < 0.001), while marginal coverage was unchanged, as guaranteed. Two design choices were essential. Computing the scores from in-sample residuals of the full calibrant fit instead of leave-one-out refits — a tempting shortcut — reduced mean coverage to {{abl.P_naive_noLOO.coverage_mean|.3f}}, an empirical confirmation that the leave-one-out construction is what makes the guarantee hold. Replacing the monotone map by a linear one increased the interval score by {{abl.P_linear_map.median_diff|.2f}} min, reflecting the curvature of RT mappings between gradients. The results were insensitive to the difficulty-model hyper-parameters: with 15 or 63 leaves and 200 or 800 trees, the mean interval score ranged from {{sens.sens_l63_e400.P.winkler|.2f}} to {{sens.sens_l15_e400.P.winkler|.2f}} min at unchanged coverage, always below that of jackknife+ (Table S10).

## Robustness to calibrant selection and calibrant errors

Calibrant selection is where the exchangeability assumption is most likely to fail in practice (Table 5, Fig. 7). When the calibrants were chosen to span the RT range, as is usual when a calibrant mix is designed, the conformal windows became slightly conservative ({{rob.span.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the proposed windows and {{rob.span.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+): spreading the calibrants left fewer test compounds outside their range ({{mech.span.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} versus {{mech.main.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} for random calibrants; Table S16), whereas the leave-one-out residuals of the extreme calibrants are themselves extrapolations. Noisy calibrant RTs (Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 2 % of the RT span) and a single outlying calibrant (shifted by 10 % of the span) also made the conformal windows conservative ({{rob.noise.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} and {{rob.outlier.P.coverage_mean|.3f}}), because calibrant errors inflate the calibration residuals; GP intervals remained invalid in all three scenarios ({{rob.outlier.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}–{{rob.span.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}).

The decisive failure occurred when the calibrants were the 20 compounds of each system that are most frequently measured across RepoRT, a proxy for mixes of well-known reference standards. All windows then under-covered markedly: {{rob.common.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the proposed windows, {{rob.common.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} for jackknife+, {{rob.common.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}} for the Gaussian window and {{rob.common.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}} for GP intervals. Two mechanisms were at work (Table S16). Commonly measured compounds cluster in part of the elution range, so that {{mech.common.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} of the test compounds lay outside the calibrant score range (versus {{mech.main.P.share_outside_cal_range|pct}} with random calibrants); and they are easier to predict than typical analytes, so that coverage fell even inside the calibrant range ({{mech.common.P.cov_inside|.3f}}). The difficulty model mitigated the first mechanism — coverage outside the calibrant range was {{mech.common.P.cov_outside|.3f}} with the proposed windows versus {{mech.common.B5_jackknife_plus.cov_outside|.3f}} with jackknife+ — but could not remove the bias. Calibrant mixes therefore need to span the RT range and to resemble the analytes; when a fixed selection rule is used, the correction described in the next sections is required.

## Generalisation to another base model and to HILIC

Replacing the multi-system retention-order model by a gradient-boosted regressor trained on the METLIN SMRT system only — the common "pre-train on SMRT, then project" approach — left the validity conclusions unchanged (at $n=20$: proposed {{gen.smrt.n20.P.coverage_mean|.3f}}, jackknife+ {{gen.smrt.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}}, Gaussian {{gen.smrt.n20.B1_gauss_rmse.coverage_mean|.3f}}, GP {{gen.smrt.n20.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}; Table 6) and enlarged the advantage of the proposed windows (mean interval score {{gen.smrt.n20.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} versus {{gen.smrt.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.winkler_mean|.2f}} min for jackknife+). The windows were wider than with the retention-order model (median {{gen.smrt.n20.P.width_median_min|.2f}} versus {{main.P.width_median_min|.2f}} min), consistent with the better transferability of retention order [@kretschmer2026order]. On {{gen.hilic.n20.P.n_systems|int}} HILIC systems, with base and difficulty models trained on HILIC data from other sources only, the conformal windows again had nominal coverage ({{gen.hilic.n20.P.coverage_mean|.3f}} proposed, {{gen.hilic.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.coverage_mean|.3f}} jackknife+) and the GP intervals again under-covered ({{gen.hilic.n20.B3_gp_PI.coverage_mean|.3f}}). In HILIC, however, the difficulty model increased the interval score ({{gen.hilic.n20.P.winkler_mean|.2f}} versus {{gen.hilic.n20.B5_jackknife_plus.winkler_mean|.2f}} min for jackknife+): with {{tsz.HILIC.ge30.min}}–{{tsz.HILIC.ge30.max}} heterogeneous training systems per fold, compared with {{tsz.RP.ge30.min}}–{{tsz.RP.ge30.max}} in reversed phase, the meta-learned scale function is too noisy to be useful. Together with the short-gradient results, this delimits the method: the validity of the conformal construction does not depend on the base model or the separation mode, whereas the efficiency gain of the learned normalisation requires a large and representative collection of training systems; where such a collection is lacking, plain jackknife+ should be used.

## Error analysis

With the proposed 90 % windows ($n=20$, first draw), {{err.miss_rate|pct}} of test compounds fell outside their window, and {{err.late_share|pct0}} of these misses eluted later than the window. Miss rates were highest for phenols ({{errcls.Phenols|pct}}), quinolines ({{errcls.Quinolines and derivatives|pct}}), organonitrogen compounds ({{errcls.Organonitrogen compounds|pct}}) and pyridines ({{errcls.Pyridines and derivatives|pct}}) and lowest for indoles ({{errcls.Indoles and derivatives|pct}}), fatty acyls ({{errcls.Fatty Acyls|pct}}) and steroids ({{errcls.Steroids and steroid derivatives|pct}}) (Table S9). The classes with elevated miss rates contain basic nitrogen or acidic phenolic groups whose ionisation, and hence retention, depends on mobile-phase pH and column chemistry — the selectivity changes that a monotone transfer of a system-independent score cannot represent [@malm2025projection; @kretschmer2026order].
