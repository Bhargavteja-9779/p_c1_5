# Introduction

Non-target screening (NTS) by liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS) detects thousands of features per sample, but turning a feature into a structure remains the bottleneck of exposomics, environmental monitoring and metabolomics [@hupatz2025critical]. Accurate mass and MS/MS spectra typically leave several isomeric candidate structures, so orthogonal evidence is needed [@schymanski2014identifying]. Retention time (RT) is the most widely available such evidence: a candidate whose expected RT is incompatible with the observed RT of the feature can be discarded [@hupatz2025critical; @metz2025identification].

Using RT this way requires predicting it on the laboratory's own chromatographic system, for which training data are rarely available. Public RT knowledge is therefore transferred with a small set of calibrant compounds measured on that system, either by *projection* through a monotone relation fitted on shared compounds [@stanstrup2015predret; @abatepella2015retention] and calibrant-based retention indices [@aalizadeh2021development; @stoffel2022alkylpyridinium], or by structure-based *prediction* models trained on large public collections [@domingo2019smrt; @kretschmer2024report] and adapted to the target system [@bouwmeester2020generalized; @garcia2022probabilistic; @fadil2026fastret; @xiong2026unirt]. Because elution order is far better conserved between systems than absolute RT, retention-order models learned across many systems [@bach2018retention; @kretschmer2026order] followed by a calibrant mapping now transfer without target training data. Their accuracy, however, depends strongly on the similarity of source and target systems [@malm2025projection].

For candidate filtering, the point prediction is only half of the story: a *tolerance window* must be set around it, and here practice is heuristic — fixed windows (±0.1–0.5 min [@metz2025identification], ±0.40 min [@kretschmer2026order], ±3 min [@mceachran2018comparison]), fractions of the run time [@mceachran2018comparison], or multiples of the calibration error such as ±2 RMSE [@malm2025projection]. Projection-specific windows had to be relaxed by 50 % to suit all of seven laboratories [@boswell2015calculation], and where the fraction of correct structures surviving the filter was measured, losses were substantial: 21–41 % on four external methods [@domingo2019smrt], 6–20 % after calibration with 35 calibrants [@zhang2024generic]. The asymmetry of the two errors makes this important: a window that is too wide keeps some wrong candidates, but one that is too narrow silently removes the right answer, which no downstream score can recover [@metz2025identification].

Conformal prediction offers prediction intervals with a finite-sample coverage guarantee for any underlying model, assuming exchangeable data [@vovk2005algorithmic; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]; it has recently been used for spectroscopic calibration [@wiens2026calibrated; @bhargav2026group], MS/MS retrieval [@rakhshaninejad2026reliable] and collision-cross-section (CCS) candidate prioritisation in NTS [@ferrandoplo2026candidate]. For RT on a laboratory's own system, conformal windows can be built from the calibrants themselves, e.g. by jackknife+ [@barber2021jackknife]. Two properties of such windows, however, make their guarantee weaker in practice than it reads. First, it is *marginal*: it averages over all laboratories and over all calibrant sets a laboratory might have drawn. The retention achieved by one laboratory with its actual calibrants fluctuates around the nominal level, and a guarantee for the laboratory's own calibrant set (training-conditional or PAC validity) needs hundreds of calibration points when the calibration set is the only source of information [@vovk2013conditional; @bian2023training]. Second, conformal windows from $n$ calibrants are finite only if $n\ge 1/\alpha-1$, and they are valid only if the calibrants resemble the analytes, whereas laboratories use few calibrants, chosen by availability, frequency of use or a fixed standard mix [@aalizadeh2021development; @malm2024norman].

Both limitations stem from treating the laboratory's calibrants as the only calibration data. Public repositories, however, now contain RTs measured on hundreds of chromatographic systems [@kretschmer2024report]. On every such system the laboratory's calibrant protocol — how many calibrants, chosen by which rule — can be replayed, and the retention it would have achieved there can be measured on the system's remaining compounds. Treating *systems*, rather than compounds, as the exchangeable calibration units turns the question "which window multiplier gives this laboratory at least $1-\varepsilon$ retention with confidence $1-\delta$?" into an ordinary conformal quantile over hundreds of systems. Related constructions exist in machine learning — PAC prediction sets for meta-learning [@park2022pac], hierarchical conformal prediction [@dunn2023distribution; @lee2026hierarchical] and few-shot conformal prediction with auxiliary tasks [@fisch2021fewshot; @park2024fewshot] — but, to our knowledge, they have not been developed for chromatographic retention or used to give a laboratory a guarantee about its own RT filter. The closest analytical work calibrates across laboratories by other means. A hierarchical Bayesian model harmonises collision cross sections across platforms from three or more reference compounds, with average coverage of its intervals [@zhou2026unified]. Calibrated RT filters reduce candidate lists at a stated average retention of true structures on a few methods [@lai2027tctoport]. Neither gives a guarantee for the individual laboratory. Neither accounts for how the laboratory selected its calibrants, and neither tells it which calibrants to measure.

Here we develop *cross-system conformal calibration* (XSC) of RT windows and evaluate it as an analytical tool. Specifically, we (i) show on 228 reversed-phase systems from RepoRT, in leave-source-out validation, that windows with a marginal guarantee — including conformal jackknife+ windows — leave about a third of laboratories below the intended retention with 20 calibrants; (ii) derive XSC windows whose multiplier is calibrated on public systems with the laboratory's own protocol replayed, which guarantee that a laboratory retains at least $1-\varepsilon$ of the correct structures with probability at least $1-\delta$, remain finite with as few as three calibrants, and stabilise the laboratory's error scale by shrinkage towards a meta-learned difficulty model; (iii) show that replaying the calibrant-selection rule keeps the guarantee when calibrants are not representative (RT-spanning, most common or most polar compounds); and (iv) quantify the analytical consequence on real candidate lists — all isomers of each analyte in PubChemLite for Exposomics [@schymanski2021pubchemlite] — and on 37 NTS methods of the NORMAN interlaboratory comparison [@malm2025projection], including their own calibration mix. A related method of ours for near-infrared calibration under deployment shift [@bhargav2026group] calibrates intervals on labelled samples from each new group; it does not address chromatographic transfer, laboratory-level guarantees or candidate filtering.
